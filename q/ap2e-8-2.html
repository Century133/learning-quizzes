8.2 Bones of the Upper Limb

Proximal humerus features

This section identifies the landmarks of the upper humerus including the head, necks, and tubercles.

Key terms

  1. Head of the humerus — The large, round, smooth region that faces medially and articulates with the glenoid cavity of the scapula.
  2. Anatomical neck — The narrow margin located immediately around the smooth articular surface of the humeral head.
  3. Greater tubercle — An expanded bony area located on the lateral side of the proximal humerus that serves as a muscle attachment site.
  4. Lesser tubercle — A smaller bony area found on the anterior aspect of the proximal humerus for muscle attachment.
  5. Surgical neck — The region at the base of the proximal end of the humerus that is a common site for arm fractures.

Muscle tendon pathways

This section describes the specific bony groove on the humerus that facilitates the passage of muscle tendons.

Key terms

  1. Intertubercular groove (bicipital groove) — A narrow space passing between the greater and lesser tubercles of the humerus.

1.1 Biceps brachii tendon — The specific connective tissue structure that passes through the intertubercular groove to reach the arm.

Elbow joint mechanics

This section details the articulations between the humerus, radius, and ulna, including the depressions that accommodate movement.

Key terms

  1. Trochlea — A spindle- or pulley-shaped region on the distal humerus that articulates with the ulna.
  2. Capitulum — A knob-like structure on the anterior surface of the distal humerus that articulates with the radius.
  3. Coronoid fossa — A depression above the trochlea that receives the coronoid process of the ulna when the elbow is flexed.
  4. Radial fossa — A small depression above the capitulum that receives the head of the radius during elbow flexion.
  5. Olecranon fossa — A large depression on the posterior humerus that receives the olecranon process of the ulna during full extension.
  6. Trochlear notch — A large, C-shaped region on the proximal ulna that resembles a crescent wrench and articulates with the trochlea.

Upper limb fractures

This section summarizes common fractures of the humerus, radius, and carpal bones as described in clinical contexts.

Key terms

  1. Impacted fracture — An injury often caused by a fall onto the hand or elbow that drives the humeral shaft into the surgical neck.
  2. Supracondylar fracture — A fracture across the distal humerus above the epicondyles, common in children who fall on the tip of the elbow.
  3. Intercondylar fracture — A break between the epicondyles that separates one or both from the main body of the humerus.
  4. Colles fracture — A complete transverse fracture of the distal radius that drives the separated fragment posteriorly and superiorly.

4.1 "Dinner fork" bend — A characteristic forearm deformity caused by the posterior displacement of the hand during a distal radius fracture.

  1. Scaphoid fracture — A break in the most commonly injured carpal bone, often initially misdiagnosed as a wrist sprain.

5.1 Bone necrosis — The death of bone tissue that may occur following a scaphoid fracture due to a naturally poor blood supply.

Muscle attachment sites

This section lists the specific roughened regions and processes where muscles and ligaments anchor to the upper limb bones.

Key terms

  1. Deltoid tuberosity — A roughened, V-shaped region on the lateral side of the humeral shaft where the deltoid muscle attaches.
  2. Medial epicondyle — A prominent projection on the distal humerus that serves as the origin for powerful anterior forearm grasping muscles.
  3. Lateral epicondyle — A small projection on the distal humerus where the weaker posterior forearm muscles attach.
  4. Ulnar tuberosity — A roughened area on the anterior ulna located just below the coronoid process.
  5. Radial tuberosity — An oval-shaped bony protuberance on the medial side of the proximal radius that serves as a muscle attachment point.
  6. Styloid process of the radius — A pointed projection that provides ligament attachment and limits the range of motion for lateral deviations.

Forearm bone stability

This section explains the structural components that keep the radius and ulna united along their length.

Key terms

  1. Interosseous border — A small ridge running along the medial side of the radius and the lateral side of the ulna.
  2. Interosseous membrane of the forearm — A sheet of dense connective tissue that unites the radius and ulna bones along their shafts.

Wrist joint movement

This section defines the specific joints and carpal bone groups responsible for hand movement at the wrist.

Key terms

  1. Radiocarpal joint — The articulation between the distal radius and specific proximal carpal bones that forms the wrist joint.

1.1 Scaphoid, lunate, and triquetrum — Carpal bones where the radius articulates with the first two directly and the third via a fibrocartilaginous pad.

  1. Midcarpal joint — The articulation located between the proximal and distal rows of carpal bones.

Upper limb alignment

This section describes the anatomical deviation of the forearm relative to the arm in the anatomical position.

Key terms

  1. Carrying angle — A lateral deviation of 5–15 degrees of the forearm, which is larger in females to accommodate a wider pelvis.

Carpal tunnel function

This section defines the boundaries and the contents of the passageway into the hand through the wrist.

Key terms

  1. Flexor retinaculum — A strong ligament that spans the carpal bones to form the roof of the carpal tunnel.
  2. Carpal tunnel — A passageway formed by carpal bones and a ligament through which nine muscle tendons and a major nerve enter the hand.
  3. Carpal tunnel syndrome — A condition where inflammation within the carpal tunnel compresses the nerve, causing pain, numbness, and muscle weakness.

Hand gripping mechanics

This section details the role of the metacarpal bones in enhancing the strength and stability of the grip.

Key terms

  1. Metacarpal bones — Five elongated bones forming the palm of the hand, numbered 1–5 starting from the thumb side.
  2. Power gripping — The action of pulling the medial metacarpal bones anteriorly to increase contact and strength during a firm grip.

2.1 Fourth and fifth metacarpals — Palm bones with limited mobility, though the fifth bone exhibits significantly more motion than the fourth.

Thumb mobility

This section explains the anatomical uniqueness and skeletal structure of the first digit.

Key terms

  1. First metacarpal bone — The bone at the base of the thumb that is separated from other metacarpals to allow independent movement.
  2. Pollex — The anatomical term for the thumb, which is specifically identified as digit number 1.

2.1 Proximal and distal phalanges — The two specific bones that make up the complete skeletal structure of the thumb.

Forearm joint articulation

This section identifies the specific joints where the radius and ulna articulate to allow rotation.

Key terms

  1. Proximal radioulnar joint — The articulation between the head of the radius and the radial notch of the ulna.
  2. Distal radioulnar joint — The articulation between the head of the ulna and the ulnar notch of the radius.
