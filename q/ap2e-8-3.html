8.3 The Pelvic Girdle and Pelvis

Pelvic stability

The pelvic bones are strongly united to form a largely immobile structure that provides a stable weight-bearing foundation for the upper body.

Key terms

  1. Pelvic girdle — A single hip bone that serves as the primary attachment point for the lower limb.
  2. Pelvis — The complete bony structure composed of the right and left hip bones, the sacrum, and the coccyx.
    1. Axial connection — A firm attachment to the sacrum that allows body weight to be transferred laterally from the vertebral column.

Sitting mechanics

The posterior and inferior regions of the pelvic girdle are structurally specialized to support the mass of the body during seated positions.

Key terms

  1. Ischium — The posteroinferior region of the hip bone that supports the body while sitting.
  2. Ischial tuberosity — A large, roughened area of the inferior ischium that carries the body's weight when seated.

Muscle attachment

Various spines and roughened surfaces across the hip bone provide essential anchoring sites for the muscles of the trunk and lower limbs.

Key terms

  1. Anterior superior iliac spine — The rounded anterior termination of the iliac crest that serves as an attachment for thigh muscles.
  2. Anterior inferior iliac spine — A rounded protuberance inferior to the iliac crest used for the attachment of thigh muscles.
  3. Posterior superior iliac spine — The posterior termination of the iliac crest where muscles and very strong ligaments of the sacroiliac joint attach.
  4. Posterior inferior iliac spine — A bony point at the inferior end of the auricular surface where muscles and strong supporting ligaments attach.
  5. Ischial tuberosity — A large, roughened area on the ischium that provides an attachment point for the posterior thigh muscles.
  6. Obturator foramen — A large opening that provides internal and external surfaces for muscle attachment.

Joint formation

The pelvic structure contains specialized articulations where bones converge to facilitate lateral weight transfer and form the hip socket.

Key terms

  1. Sacroiliac joint — A largely immobile joint formed by the articulation between the auricular surfaces of the ilium and the sacrum.
  2. Pubic symphysis — The specialized anterior joint where the right and left pubic portions of the hip bones converge.
  3. Acetabulum — A deep, cup-shaped cavity on the lateral hip bone that forms the socket for the hip joint.
    1. Ilium — The fan-like superior region that contributes to the superior portion of the acetabulum.
    2. Ischium — The posteroinferior region of the hip bone that contributes to the posterior portion of the acetabulum.
    3. Pubis — The anterior region of the hip bone that contributes to the anterior portion of the acetabulum.

Weight distribution

The immobile design of the pelvis facilitates the lateral transfer of upper body mass from the axial skeleton to the lower limbs.

Key terms

  1. Weight transfer pathway — The lateral movement of body weight from the vertebral column through the pelvic girdle and hip joints to the lower limbs.

Nerve pathways

Specific openings in the posterior pelvis are defined by notches and ligaments to allow the passage of soft tissues to the legs.

Key terms

  1. Greater sciatic foramen — The large superior opening through which muscles, nerves, and blood vessels for the lower limb exit the pelvis.
    1. Boundaries — This opening is formed by the greater sciatic notch, the sacrum, and the sacrospinous ligament.
  2. Lesser sciatic foramen — The smaller inferior opening used by muscles, nerves, and blood vessels to exit toward the lower limb.
    1. Boundaries — This opening is formed by the lesser sciatic notch, the sacrospinous ligament, and the sacrotuberous ligament.

Pelvic boundaries

The internal space of the pelvis is divided into distinct regions categorized by their relationship to the pelvic brim and outlet.

Key terms

  1. Greater pelvis — Also known as the false pelvis, this broad superior region is occupied by portions of the small and large intestines.
  2. Lesser pelvis — Also known as the true pelvis, this narrow inferior space contains the bladder and other internal pelvic organs.
  3. Pelvic brim — Also known as the pelvic inlet, this line forms the superior margin of the lesser pelvis and separates the two cavities.
    1. Anatomical borders — The line formed by the superior pubic symphysis, pectineal line, arcuate line, and sacral promontory.
  4. Pelvic outlet — The inferior limit of the lesser pelvic cavity that defines the lower opening of the pelvis.
    1. Anatomical borders — The boundary formed by the inferior pubic symphysis, ischiopubic ramus, ischial tuberosity, sacrotuberous ligament, and inferior tip of the coccyx.

Ligamentous support

Strong connective bands span the pelvic bones to ensure the sacrum remains immobile while carrying the weight of the upper body.

Key terms

  1. Anterior sacroiliac ligament — A strong band on the anterior side of the joint supporting the connection between the sacrum and ilium.
  2. Posterior sacroiliac ligament — A ligament on the posterior side that supports and helps immobilize the sacroiliac joint.
  3. Sacrospinous ligament — A band of connective tissue running from the sacrum to the ischial spine of the hip bone.
  4. Sacrotuberous ligament — A connective tissue band spanning from the sacrum to the ischial tuberosity.

Obstetric adaptation

The female pelvis exhibits structural modifications such as increased width and shallow depth to facilitate the process of childbirth.

Key terms

  1. Subpubic angle — The inverted V-shape which is greater than 80 degrees in females and less than 70 degrees in males.
  2. Pelvic inlet shape — The superior margin of the lesser pelvis, which is rounded or oval in females and heart-shaped in males.
  3. Sacrum — A female structure that is wider, shorter, and less curved than the thicker and more curved male sacrum.
  4. Lesser pelvic cavity — A female space that is wider and more shallow than the narrower, deeper, and tapering male cavity.

Sex determination

The distinct morphological differences between male and female hip bones allow specialized professionals to accurately identify human remains.

Names and studies

  1. Forensic anthropologist — A specialist who identifies human remains by approximating age, sex, race, and height using skeletal and dental evidence.
  2. Forensic pathologist — A physician who determines the final cause of death using all collected data, including information provided by forensic anthropologists.

Pelvic orientation

When standing in the standard anatomical position, the pelvis is positioned with a specific tilt to align structural landmarks.

Key terms

  1. Anterior tilt — The alignment where the anterior superior iliac spines and pubic tubercles lie in the same vertical plane.

Bone development

The adult hip bone is a single coxal bone that results from the chronological fusion of three separate developmental regions.

Key terms

  1. Hip bone — A composite bone formed by the fusion of the ilium, ischium, and pubis during the late teenage years.

Organ containment

The cavities formed by the pelvic bones provide a protective enclosure for the digestive and reproductive systems.

Key terms

  1. Greater pelvis — The broad superior space that is more closely associated with the abdominal cavity and houses the intestines.
  2. Lesser pelvis — The narrow inferior space that contains the bladder and other internal pelvic organs.

Bony landmarks

Specific ridges and projections on the pelvic bones serve as critical points for anatomical identification and clinical orientation.

Key terms

  1. Iliac crest — The arching superior margin of the ilium that can be palpated at the waistline.
  2. Iliac fossa — The shallow depression located on the internal or anteromedial surface of the upper ilium.
  3. Arcuate line — The ridge on the ilium representing the pronounced change in curvature between its upper and lower portions.
  4. Ischial spine — A bony projection separating the greater sciatic notch from the lesser sciatic notch.
  5. Pubic tubercle — A small bump located on the superior aspect of the pubic body.
  6. Sacral promontory — The anterior margin of the superior sacrum that helps define the pelvic brim.

Anterior articulation

The forward-facing portion of the pelvis consists of a midline joint and an arched structure formed by the pubic bones.

Key terms

  1. Pubic symphysis — The specialized joint formed by the union of the right and left pubic bodies.
  2. Pubic arch — The bony structure composed of the pubic symphysis, pubic bodies, and inferior pubic rami.
  3. Subpubic angle — The inverted V-shape formed where the ischiopubic rami from both sides meet at the pubic symphysis.

Connective attachments

The pelvis utilizes a combination of bone and soft tissue layers to provide additional surfaces for muscular anchoring.

Key terms

  1. Obturator foramen — A large anteroinferior opening in the hip bone that is largely filled by a layer of connective tissue for muscle attachment.
