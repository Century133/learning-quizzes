Concubine drill

This topic details Sun Tzu's practical demonstration of military discipline and command authority using palace women.

Key terms

  1. Command authority: The absolute power and obligation of a general to enforce unambiguous instructions and compel obedience across all ranks.
    • Unclear instructions being the general's fault: A failure condition occurring when words of command are vague or orders are not thoroughly understood by the troops.
    • Clear instructions disobeyed being the officers' fault: A failure condition occurring when orders are plain and explicitly understood, yet the troops fail to comply.

Names and studies

  1. Ssu-ma Ch'ien's account of Sun Tzu and King Ho Lu:
    • What was done: Sun Tzu subjected 180 palace ladies to a military drill, dividing them into two companies commanded by King Ho Lu’s two favorite concubines, after explicitly explaining directional movements and setting up battle-axes.
    • What came out: The women broke into repeated fits of laughter upon hearing the drum signals; Sun Tzu clarified the orders, attributed the initial failure to himself, and upon the second breakdown of discipline, ordered the execution of the two favorite concubines despite the King's protestations, subsequently re-drilling the remaining ladies into silent, flawless precision.
    • What it showed: Operational commands, once issued under royal commission, must be strictly enforced regardless of political favor or sovereign interference to achieve absolute discipline.

Sun Pin

This topic covers the life, military genius, and textual historical identity of Sun Tzu’s descendant.

Key terms

  1. Mutilation nickname: The appellation "Pin," meaning foot amputation, bestowed upon Sun Wu's descendant after he suffered bodily mutilation during his career.

Names and studies

  1. Ssu-ma Ch'ien's biography of Sun Pin:
    • What was done: Ssu-ma Ch'ien recorded the life of Sun Pin, noting that he continued to discuss and analyze the art of war even after having his feet cut off, and later directed the military operations that crushed his treacherous rival P'ang Chuan.
    • What came out: Sun Pin established a towering historical reputation as an outstanding strategist and military genius in his own right, distinct from his ancestor.
    • What it showed: The historical distinction between Sun Wu (the elder Sun Tzu) and Sun Pin, demonstrating a multi-generational lineage of strategic intellect while confirming Sun Pin's separate identity in early Chinese military literature.

Historical dates

This topic chronologically outlines key historical milestones in the warfare between Wu, Ch'u, and Yüeh.

Key terms

  1. Spring and Autumn period warfare timeline: The primary chronological framework establishing the geopolitical struggles and operational milestones of the Wu State.
    • 514 B.C. — Accession of Ho Lu to the throne of the Wu State.
    • 512 B.C. — First explicit mention of Sun Wu serving as a general during King Ho Lu's campaign against Ch'u.
    • 506 B.C. — Decisive battle of Po-chu, the capture of the Ch'u capital Ying, and the final historical mention of Sun Wu in the Shih Chi.
    • 496 B.C. — Death of King Ho Lu from an injury sustained at the battle of Tsui-li.
    • 473 B.C. — Final defeat, collapse, and complete extinction of the Wu State by Kou Chien, King of Yüeh.

Classical statecraft

This topic covers traditional Chinese philosophical and political principles regarding the necessity and regulation of military force.

Key terms

  1. Statecraft duality: The balance maintained by enlightened rulers between preserving civil peace as a norm and exercising military force when driven by necessity.
    • Ssu-ma Fa: Canonical treatise establishing the legalist moral justification for force, noting that if one man slays another of set purpose, he himself may rightfully be slain.
    • Lun Yu: Canonical Confucian text emphasizing the civil governance imperative that a state must maintain a sufficiency of military strength.
    • Shu Ching: Classic text highlighting that the military ("the army") is formally listed among the eight essential objects of governmental administration.

Names and studies

  1. Jan Yu's defense of military study to Chi-sun:
    • What was done: Jan Yu was questioned by Chi-sun regarding how he, as a direct disciple of Confucius, could actively study and practice the military arts.
    • What came out: Jan Yu affirmed that Confucius himself taught both civil governance and military functions, noting that true sages integrate warlike readiness with moral governance.
    • What it showed: Classical Chinese statecraft does not isolate military preparedness from ethical rule, proving that strategic study was an integral component of comprehensive governance.

Tso Chuan omission

This topic addresses the historical controversy surrounding Sun Wu's complete absence from the primary contemporary chronicle.

Key terms

  1. Historiographical silence: The critical problem posed by the total absence of Sun Wu's name from the Tso Chuan, the definitive contemporary historical commentary of the Spring and Autumn period.

Names and studies

  1. Yeh Shui-hsin's textual critique:
    • What was done: Scholar Yeh Shui-hsin systematically examined the Tso Chuan, cross-referencing its entries against the biographical claims made by Ssu-ma Ch'ien regarding Sun Wu's triumphs.
    • What came out: He revealed that while the Tso Chuan recorded minor plebeians, hireling ruffians, and Sun Wu's contemporaries (such as Wu Yuan and Po P'ei), it completely omitted Sun Wu, his military appointment, and his alleged participation in taking Ying.
    • What it showed: Yeh argued that Sun Wu was not an active historical general, suggesting that his recorded exploits were theoretical fabrications created by later scholars and disciples.

Chronology of conflict

This topic uses internal text evidence regarding geopolitical rivalries to deduce the probable period of the text's composition.

Key terms

  1. Internal textual evidence: Specific passages within the 13 chapters that reference real-time geopolitical conditions and state animosities to determine date of authorship.
    • Mention of Yüeh's troop superiority (VI. § 21): Textual statement evaluating the numerical abundance of Yüeh forces compared to Wu.
    • Mutual hatred of Wu and Yüeh men (XI. § 30): Textual passage citing the deep, active hostility between the populaces of Wu and Yüeh as a standard operational illustration.

Names and studies

  1. Lionel Giles' chronological analysis:
    • What was done: Translator Lionel Giles correlated the explicit textual references to Yüeh in The Art of War with the documented historical dates of Wu's successive wars against Ch'u and Yüeh.
    • What came out: Giles demonstrated that the text completely ignores Ch'u as the primary antagonist and instead focuses on Yüeh as the chief menace, a condition that only arose after Ch'u's humiliation in 506 B.C.
    • What it showed: Placed the probable date of the book's composition within the historical lull between 505 B.C. and King Ho Lu's death in 496 B.C.

Commentators on Sun Tzu

This topic surveys the classical scholars and military leaders who annotated and preserved the text.

Key terms

  1. Exegetical tradition: The historic lineage of military strategists, poets, and historians who edited, clarified, and interpreted the obscure maxims of The Art of War.
    • Ts'ao Ts'ao (Wei Wu Ti): The earliest known commentator (155–220 A.D.), renowned for his austere, compressed, and authoritative notes.
    • Tu Mu: Celebrated T'ang dynasty poet and historian whose expansive commentary provides rich historical parallels and context.
    • Mei Yao-ch'en: Sung dynasty scholar praised by Ou-yang Hsiu for unearthing the underlying logical sequence and deep meaning of Sun Tzu's terse sentences.
    • Chang Yu: Later commentator who provided masterly, highly lucid expansions of Ts'ao Ts'ao's brief notes.

Names and studies

  1. Sun Hsing-yen's textual recension:
    • What was done: Scholar Sun Hsing-yen discovered Chi T'ien-pao's ancient commentary text at Hua-yin and systematically collated it with complete fragments preserved in the T'ang dynasty T'ung Tien and the Sung dynasty T'ai P'ing Yu Lan.
    • What came out: He corrected centuries of accumulated textual corruptions, errors, and omissions, cutting woodblocks to publish a clean, restored edition.
    • What it showed: Produced what is accepted as the "standard text" and the closest modern approximation to Sun Tzu's original 13 chapters.

Fundamental factors

This topic outlines the five core principles that govern military strategy and field deliberations.

Key terms

  1. Constant factors: The five fundamental dimensions that must be calculated and evaluated during temple deliberations before engaging in military operations.
    • The Moral Law — The principle causing the populace to be in complete alignment and harmony with their ruler, driving them to follow him regardless of danger.
    • Heaven — The natural elements and temporal cycles, encompassing night and day, cold and heat, times and seasons.
    • Earth — The physical terrain features, comprising distances, danger and security, open ground and narrow passes, and the chances of life and death.
    • The Commander — The requisite personal attributes of leadership, defined by the five virtues: wisdom, sincerity, benevolence, courage, and strictness.
    • Method and discipline — The operational mechanics of an army, including correct marshaling of subdivisions, rank gradations, supply line maintenance, and military expenditure control.

Strategic priorities

This topic presents the ranked hierarchy of offensive strategic options in war.

Key terms

  1. Hierarchy of attack: The foundational doctrine ordering strategic offensive priorities from highest excellence to worst policy.
    • Baulking the enemy's plans: The highest form of generalship, consisting of actively counter-attacking and thwarting the adversary's strategy before it materializes.
    • Preventing the junction of enemy forces: The second-best strategy, focused on isolating the enemy from his allies and preventing force concentration.
    • Attacking the enemy's army in the field: The third option, undertaken when the adversary is already fully deployed and concentrated.
    • Besieging walled cities: The lowest and worst policy, which drains resources, consumes months of preparation, and results in heavy losses.

Deception and tactics

This topic covers the fundamental reliance on artifice, feints, and dual tactical methods to achieve victory.

Key terms

  1. Warfare deception: The core axiom stating that all warfare is grounded in artifice, requiring a commander to feign incapacity, inactivity, or distance to manipulate enemy perceptions.
  2. Tactical duality: The continuous, complementary interplay between two primary engagement methods on the battlefield.
    • Direct method (cheng): The conventional, direct operational approach utilized primarily to join battle and fix the enemy.
    • Indirect method (ch'i): The unconventional, maneuvering operational approach utilized to execute flank or rear attacks and secure decisive victory.

Names and studies

  1. Sun Pin's stove-reduction maneuver at Wei:
    • What was done: Operating against Wei general P'ang Chuan, Sun Pin exploited the local stereotype that his Ch'i troops were cowards by ordering 100,000 campfires on the first night in Wei territory, 50,000 on the second night, and only 20,000 on the third.
    • What came out: P'ang Chuan concluded that over half of Sun Pin's army had deserted out of fear, causing him to abandon his heavy infantry and pursue rapidly with light troops into a narrow defile at night.
    • What it showed: Sun Pin ambushed and annihilated P'ang Chuan at the defile, demonstrating how feigning weakness and manipulating indicators tricks an adversary into total destruction.

Forced marches

This topic details the severe physical and operational risks of rapid long-distance troop movements to gain an advantage.

Key terms

  1. Distance-loss ratio: The inevitable rate of attrition, leader loss, and force dispersal suffered when pushing troops through forced marches to snatch a tactical advantage.
    • 100 li march — A forced march over this distance results in the leaders of all three army divisions falling into enemy hands, with only one-tenth of the force reaching the destination.
    • 50 li march — A forced march over this distance results in the loss of the leader of the first division, with only half the force arriving intact.
    • 30 li march — A forced march over this distance results in two-thirds of the army arriving at the goal.

General's flaws

This topic details the five personal character vulnerabilities that bring ruin upon a military commander.

Key terms

  1. Dangerous faults: The five critical psychological vulnerabilities in a general's character that threaten army operations and lead to disaster.
    • Recklessness — Blind bravery without forethought, which exposes the commander to ambush and destruction.
    • Cowardice — An excessive preoccupation with self-preservation, leading to hesitation and capture.
    • Hasty temper — An easily provoked, choleric disposition that can be manipulated through deliberate insults.
    • Delicacy of honour — An over-sensitivity to shame and slanderous reports, making the general vulnerable to provocation.
    • Over-solicitude for men — Excessive worry over the immediate comfort of the soldiers, which distracts from broader strategic necessities and causes operational paralysis.

Names and studies

  1. Teng Ch'iang's provocation of Yao Hsing (Hsiang):
    • What was done: Facing Yao Hsing (Hsiang), who remained defensively entrenched behind his walls, general Teng Ch'iang recognized his opponent's choleric disposition and launched continuous, annoying sallies against the fortifications while executing feigned retreats.
    • What came out: Yao Hsing (Hsiang) lost his temper, abandoned his fortified defensive position to give chase, and fell into a prepared ambush at San-yuan where his force was destroyed.
    • What it showed: A general's hasty temper can be deliberately weaponized by an opponent to lure him out of strong positions into total defeat.

Observing signs

This topic focuses on reading environmental indicators and enemy behaviors to deduce hidden movements and conditions.

Key terms

  1. Field indicators: Physical clues and behavioral shifts in the environment that reveal an adversary's unmanifested tactical intentions and physical state.
    • Moving trees — Trees shaking across a forest signify that the enemy is clearing a passage and advancing.
    • Rising birds — Birds suddenly shooting upward in flight indicate an ambuscade concealed immediately beneath them.
    • High, sharp dust columns — High, pointed dust plumes indicate the approach of heavy war-chariots moving in single file.
    • Low, broad dust clouds — Low dust spreading across a wide area betokens the advance of infantry marching abreast.
    • Whispering in small knots — Officers and soldiers speaking in subdued tones or gathering in small groups points to widespread disaffection among the rank and file.

Names and studies

  1. T'ien Tan's deception at Chi-mo:
    • What was done: While besieged at Chi-mo, T'ien Tan sent envoys offering surrender, collected silver from citizens to bribe the enemy general, placed women and weak men on the walls, and hid his elite troops while preparing 1,000 decorated cattle fitted with fiery rush-tails and horn-blades.
    • What came out: The besieging Yen army grew relaxed, arrogant, and completely slack; T'ien Tan then breached the walls at night, unleashed the maddened cattle, and followed with 5,000 picked warriors to completely rout the terrified enemy.
    • What it showed: Displays of humility, false surrender, and simulated weakness serve to induce enemy carelessness prior to launching a decisive counter-offensive.

River tactics

This topic dictates positioning and engagement rules when fighting near or across river obstacles.

Key terms

  1. River engagement rules: Tactical imperatives governing force deployment and engagement timing near water obstacles.
    • Mid-stream trap — A tactical rule dictating that when an invading force crosses a river, the defender must not meet it in mid-stream, but rather allow half the army to cross before delivering a decisive attack.
    • Upstream advantage — A positioning rule requiring craft to be anchored higher up the stream than the adversary, facing the sun, and forbidding any movement directly against the current.

Names and studies

  1. Han Hsin's damming of the Wei River:
    • What was done: Facing Lung Chu across the Wei River, Han Hsin secretly placed 10,000 sandbags upstream to dam the river, led a vanguard across to attack Lung Chu, feigned defeat, and retreated across the ford to lure the enemy into pursuit.
    • What came out: As Lung Chu's force pursued across the riverbed, Han Hsin ordered the sandbag dam cut open; the surging water swept down, cutting the enemy force in two and leaving Lung Chu's isolated vanguard to be completely annihilated.
    • What it showed: Controlling and manipulating river flow during an enemy transit provides immense tactical leverage to split and destroy superior numbers.

Terrain types

This topic categorizes six ground formations and the tactical maneuvers appropriate to each.

Key terms

  1. Ground classification: The systematic breakdown of six environmental terrain formations and their corresponding operational rules.
    • Accessible ground — Terrain easily traversed by both sides; secure elevated, sunny positions first and guard supply lines.
    • Entangling ground — Ground easy to abandon but difficult to re-occupy; sally forth to attack only if the enemy is unprepared.
    • Temporising ground — Position where neither side gains by moving first; retreat to entice the enemy out, then strike his exposed column.
    • Narrow passes — Chokepoints to be occupied first and strongly garrisoned; do not attack if the enemy holds them fully garrisoned.
    • Precipitous heights — Elevated terrain where a commander must occupy sunny spots first; if the enemy holds them, retreat to lure him away.
    • Positions at a great distance — Ground where armies face each other across vast distances; initiating long marches exhausts troops and causes operational disadvantage.

Names and studies

  1. P'ei Hsing-chien's camp relocation:
    • What was done: During a punitive campaign, general P'ei Hsing-chien fully entrenched his army camp on flat ground, but suddenly ordered his reluctant officers to shift the entire camp to a nearby hill despite loud protests over troop fatigue.
    • What came out: That night, a severe rainstorm flooded the original lower camp site to a depth of over twelve feet, vindicating the general's foresight.
    • What it showed: Securing elevated ground is vital not only for tactical superiority over enemies but also for protecting an army against natural hazards.

Command failures

This topic outlines six severe army calamities resulting directly from negligence or incompetence in leadership.

Key terms

  1. Six Calamities: The structural failure conditions in an army caused not by natural environmental factors, but by the operational errors of the commander.
    • Flight — The failure caused by hurling an army against an enemy force ten times its size, resulting in immediate retreat.
    • Insubordination — The breakdown occurring when common soldiers are disciplined and strong, but their officers are weak and incapable of exerting authority.
    • Collapse — The condition where officers are energetic and strong, but the common rank-and-file soldiers are physically weak and feeble.
    • Ruin — The catastrophe caused by high-ranking officers who are angry, insubordinate, and deliver battle independently out of personal resentment before orders are given.
    • Disorganisation — The chaos resulting when a general is weak, his commands are vague, duties are unassigned, and troops form ranks in a slovenly manner.
    • Rout — The disaster happening when a commander fails to estimate enemy strength, hurls weak units against strong ones, and neglects to place picked troops in the front rank.

Names and studies

  1. T'ien Pu's command breakdown at Wei:
    • What was done: T'ien Pu took command of an army at Wei to attack Wang T'ing-ts'ou, but exhibited a total lack of authority; his soldiers held him in contempt and openly flouted discipline by riding donkeys through the camp by the thousands.
    • What came out: When T'ien Pu attempted to engage the enemy, his insubordinate troops turned tail, broke ranks, and dispersed completely, driving T'ien Pu to commit suicide.
    • What it showed: A general's weakness and inability to enforce basic discipline and command authority inevitably causes total operational collapse and rout.

Nine Situations

This topic establishes strategic maneuvers suited to nine distinct stages of territory penetration and operational urgency.

Key terms

  1. Varieties of ground: The tactical classification of nine operational environments based on penetration depth, terrain constraints, and strategic risk.
    • Dispersive ground — Fighting in one's own home territory; do not fight, as troops easily scatter toward their homes.
    • Facile ground — Shallow penetration into hostile land; do not halt, as retreat remains too easy.
    • Contentious ground — Ground offering equal strategic advantage to either side; do not attack if the enemy already holds it.
    • Open ground — Land where both sides have equal liberty of movement; do not attempt to block the enemy's way.
    • Ground of intersecting highways — Key territory adjoining three contiguous states; consolidate alliances with neighboring princes to secure it.
    • Serious ground — Deep penetration into enemy territory leaving fortified cities behind; gather plunder to support the army.
    • Difficult ground — Traversing treacherous terrain like forests, mountains, and marshes; keep pushing steadily on without encamping.
    • Hemmed-in ground — Terrain reached through narrow gorges with tortuous retreat routes; rely on stratagem to escape.
    • Desperate ground — Ground where an army is saved from destruction only by fighting immediately without delay; fight with absolute desperation.

Names and studies

  1. Kao Huan's escape at Erh-chu Chao:
    • What was done: Surrounded on hemmed-in ground by a massive opposing army under Erh-chu Chao, Kao Huan took his small force of 30,000 foot and 2,000 horse and deliberately roped together oxen and donkeys to block his own remaining escape paths.
    • What came out: Seeing that no retreat was possible, Kao Huan's soldiers fought with fierce, desperate exaltation, completely breaking and crumbling the larger opposing army.
    • What it showed: Cutting off all means of escape transforms hemmed-in ground into desperate ground, forcing soldiers to fight with maximum intensity to achieve victory.

Information control

This topic covers maintaining absolute operational security by keeping both one's own troops and the enemy in complete ignorance.

Key terms

  1. Operational secrecy: The principle of absolute information control, requiring a general to keep his plans, movements, and intentions concealed from both his adversary and his own troops.
    • Mystifying troops — Deceiving officers and men through altered plans, circuitous routes, and false reports to maintain total ignorance of strategic designs.
    • Decisive commitment — Burning boats, breaking cooking-pots, and driving troops into deep territory like a flock of sheep so that no return is possible.
    • Frontier lockdown — Closing border passes, destroying official passage tallies, and halting all emissaries upon taking command to prevent intelligence leaks.

Names and studies

  1. Pan Ch'ao's campaign against Yarkand:
    • What was done: Outnumbered by a combined force from Kutcha and Yarkand, Pan Ch'ao held a council of war and announced a false plan to disperse his army in opposite directions at night, then intentionally released local captives to leak the story to the enemy.
    • What came out: The opposing commanders divided their forces to intercept the false retreats; once they had departed, Pan Ch'ao concentrated his host and launched a surprise attack on Yarkand's camp, slaying 5,000 men and forcing Yarkand to capitulate.
    • What it showed: Keeping one's own forces ignorant while feeding leaked false intentions to the enemy successfully disperses the opposing force for a decisive strike.

Fire attacks

This topic details the five methods of environmental incendiary warfare and their tactical follow-ups.

Key terms

  1. Incendiary targets: The five specific environmental and logistical targets vulnerable to destruction by fire attack.
    • Burning soldiers in their camp: Targeting enemy personnel directly within their encampment.
    • Burning stores and supplies: Destroying the enemy's accumulated food and provisions.
    • Burning baggage trains: Targeting the transport vehicles and equipment supporting the army.
    • Burning arsenals and magazines: Destroying the enemy's weapons, armor, and munitions storage.
    • Dropping fire among the enemy: Throwing incendiary materials directly into enemy lines and positions.

Spies

This topic details the critical reliance on intelligence gathering and the deployment of secret agents in military operations.

Key terms

  1. Intelligence conversion: The process of recruiting and deploying specific classes of agents to manipulate information and uncover enemy plans.
    • Converted spy — An enemy agent or hostile native who has been captured or bought over, subsequently used to carry back false directions or expose the adversary's hidden plots.

Names and studies

  1. Pan Ch'ao's interrogation of the Shan-shan native:
    • What was done: Upon arriving at Shan-shan, Pan Ch'ao noticed the local King grew cold and negligent; suspecting enemy involvement, he trapped a local native into admitting that envoys from the hostile Hsiung-nu had recently arrived.
    • What came out: Having converted the native's information to confirm the plot, Pan Ch'ao locked up his informant, gathered his 36 officers, and executed a bold night raid that wiped out the Hsiung-nu delegation and secured the King's allegiance.
    • What it showed: Converting local intelligence by interrogating natives breaks through deceptive political appearances and exposes critical enemy threats before they materialize.
