Scapular attachment

The scapula is suspended on the posterior thorax through complex muscular attachments rather than through direct articulation with the ribs of the thoracic cage.

Key terms

  1. Scapula — A flat, triangular bone located on the posterior aspect of the shoulder.
  2. Muscular attachment — The method of support where the bone is surrounded by muscle and does not join the ribs.

Clavicle fracture

Breaks in the collarbone often result from falling on outstretched arms or lateral shoulder impacts, leading to a sagging shoulder.

Key terms

  1. Fracture mechanism — A break resulting from force exerted when falling on outstretched arms or receiving a strong blow to the lateral shoulder.
  2. Complete fracture — A break where limb weight drops the shoulder and muscles pull the lateral fragment anteriorly and medially.

Costoclavicular ligament

This ligament provides structural support for the sternoclavicular joint by anchoring the bone to the first rib.

Key terms

  1. Costoclavicular ligament — A connective tissue band that spans the sternal end of the clavicle and the underlying first rib.

Scapular borders

The triangular scapula is defined by three distinct margins named according to their anatomical position within the body.

Key terms

  1. Superior border — The top margin of the scapula featuring the suprascapular notch.
  2. Medial border — The margin of the scapula that faces the vertebral column.
  3. Lateral border — The margin of the scapula that faces the arm and ends at the glenoid cavity.

Pectoral girdle mobility

Extensive range of motion in the upper limb is enabled by the independent operation of each girdle and its single point of bony attachment.

Key terms

  1. Pectoral girdle — The two-bone structure, consisting of the scapula and clavicle, that attaches the upper limb to the axial skeleton.
  2. Mobility — The extensive range of motion enabled by the independent operation of each girdle and a single, mobile joint connection.

Scapular tubercles

Small bony projections located near the glenoid cavity serve as attachment sites for muscles of the arm.

Key terms

  1. Supraglenoid tubercle — A small bony bump located immediately above the glenoid cavity.
  2. Infraglenoid tubercle — A small bony bump located immediately below the glenoid cavity.

Limb functional demands

Upper limbs are adapted for high mobility and manipulation, whereas lower limbs are designed for weight-bearing support and stability.

Key terms

  1. Weight-bearing support — The primary function of lower limb bones, which are adapted for stability and locomotion.
  2. Mobility — The primary characteristic of the upper limbs, allowing for a wide variety of activities and object manipulation.

Glenohumeral joint

The shoulder joint is formed by the articulation of the humerus with a shallow depression on the scapula.

Key terms

  1. Glenoid cavity — The shallow depression on the scapula that receives the humerus.
  2. Glenohumeral joint — The articulation between the scapula and the arm bone (humerus).

Inferior scapular angle

The inferior angle is the lowest point of the scapula and serves as a critical attachment site for powerful muscles.

Key terms

  1. Inferior angle of the scapula — The lowest corner of the bone, located where the medial and lateral borders meet.
  2. Muscle attachment point — A site for several powerful muscles involved in executing intensive shoulder and limb movements.

Clavicle morphology

The physical characteristics of the clavicle vary based on biological sex and the level of physical activity.

Key terms

  1. Sex differences — Variations in bone shape and size between males and females.

Female: Tends to be shorter, thinner, and less curved.

Male: Heavier, longer, has a greater curvature and rougher muscle attachment surfaces.

Vascular protection

The clavicle safeguards neurovascular structures because fractures typically result in an anterior displacement of fragments that spares underlying vessels and nerves.

Key terms

  1. Protective positioning — The location of the clavicle overlying the neurovascular structures that serve the upper limb.

Sternoclavicular joint

This joint is the only point where the upper limb's pectoral girdle directly articulates with the axial skeleton.

Key terms

  1. Sternal end of the clavicle — The triangular medial end of the bone that articulates with the manubrium of the sternum.
  2. Sternoclavicular joint — The only bony articulation between the pectoral girdle and the axial skeleton.

Hand gripping mechanics

Anterior movement of the fourth and fifth metacarpals increases contact for the medial side of the hand to enhance grip strength.

Key terms

  1. Power gripping — An action where the fourth and fifth metacarpal bones move to increase contact strength.
  2. Anterior movement — The forward motion of the medial metacarpals that improves the firmness of a grip.

Upper limb fractures

Common fractures occur at specific weak points of the humerus and radius, often due to falls on an outstretched hand.

Key terms

  1. Surgical neck fracture — A common break at the base of the proximal humerus, where the head joins the shaft.
  2. Colles fracture — A complete transverse fracture across the distal radius that causes a "dinner fork" deformity.

Names and studies

Colles — described the distal radius fracture resulting in a "dinner fork" deformity.

Wrist joint movement

Motion of the hand at the wrist is the result of combined movements at two primary joint levels.

Key terms

  1. Radiocarpal joint — The articulation between the distal radius and the scaphoid, lunate, and triquetrum carpal bones.
  2. Midcarpal joint — The articulation between the proximal and distal rows of carpal bones.

Muscle tendon pathways

A strong ligamentous band creates a tunnel for tendons and nerves to transition from the forearm into the hand.

Key terms

  1. Flexor retinaculum — A strong ligament that spans the carpal bones to form the roof of the carpal tunnel.

Forearm bone stability

The radius and ulna are held together by a fibrous sheet that provides stability along their lengths.

Key terms

  1. Interosseous membrane of the forearm — A sheet of dense connective tissue that unites the radius and ulna bones.
  2. Interosseous border — The ridge along the shafts of the radius and ulna for membrane attachment.

Elbow joint mechanics

Specific depressions on the distal humerus accommodate the processes of the forearm bones during flexion and extension.

Key terms

  1. Coronoid fossa — A depression on the anterior humerus that receives the coronoid process of the ulna during flexion.
  2. Radial fossa — A depression on the anterior humerus that receives the head of the radius during flexion.
  3. Olecranon fossa — A large depression on the posterior humerus that receives the olecranon process of the ulna during extension.

Forearm joint articulation

The radius and ulna articulate with each other at both their proximal and distal ends.

Key terms

  1. Proximal radioulnar joint — The articulation between the head of the radius and the radial notch of the ulna.
  2. Distal radioulnar joint — The articulation between the head of the ulna and the ulnar notch of the radius.

Proximal humerus features

The upper end of the humerus contains landmarks for articulation with the scapula and muscle attachment.

Key terms

  1. Head of the humerus — The large, round, smooth region that articulates with the glenoid cavity of the scapula.
  2. Greater tubercle — An expanded bony area on the lateral side of the proximal humerus.
  3. Lesser tubercle — A smaller bony area found on the anterior aspect of the humerus.
  4. Intertubercular groove — A narrow sulcus between the tubercles that provides passage for a biceps brachii tendon.

Thumb mobility

The independent positioning and articulation of the first digit bone allow for a greater range of motion compared to the fingers.

Key terms

  1. First metacarpal bone — The bone at the base of the thumb that is separated from other metacarpals to allow independent motion.

Carpal tunnel function

This narrow passageway protects nine muscle tendons and a major nerve, but is susceptible to inflammation and compression.

Key terms

  1. Carpal tunnel — A passageway formed by carpal bones and the flexor retinaculum for tendons and a major nerve.
  2. Carpal tunnel syndrome — A condition where inflammation in the carpal tunnel compresses the nerve, causing pain or numbness.

Bony landmarks

Specific roughened regions and projections on long bones serve as essential sites for muscle and ligament attachment.

Key terms

  1. Deltoid tuberosity — A V-shaped region on the lateral humerus shaft for deltoid muscle attachment.
  2. Medial epicondyle — A prominent bony projection on the medial distal humerus for forearm muscle attachment.
  3. Lateral epicondyle — A smaller projection on the lateral distal humerus for posterior forearm muscle attachment.

Pelvic orientation

In the anatomical position, the pelvis is positioned with an anterior tilt.

Key terms

  1. Anterior tilt — The position where the anterior superior iliac spines and pubic tubercles lie in the same vertical plane.

Nerve pathways

Openings in the pelvic structure facilitate the passage of muscles, nerves, and blood vessels to the lower limbs.

Key terms

  1. Greater sciatic foramen — The superior pelvic opening formed by the greater sciatic notch, sacrum, and sacrospinous ligament.
  2. Lesser sciatic foramen — The inferior pelvic opening formed by the lesser sciatic notch, sacrospinous ligament, and sacrotuberous ligament.

Sex determination

Differences in pelvic morphology, particularly adaptations for childbirth, make the hip bone the primary bone for identifying biological sex.

Key terms

  1. Subpubic angle — The inverted V-shape where hip bones meet; wider in females (>80°) than males (<70°).
  2. Pelvic inlet shape — The superior margin of the lesser pelvis; rounded/oval in females, heart-shaped in males.
  3. Lesser pelvic cavity shape — The true pelvis dimensions; wider and shallower in females, narrower and deeper in males.

Weight distribution

The pelvis acts as a conduit for transferring upper body weight into the lower limbs during standing.

Key terms

  1. Weight-bearing structure — The functional role of the pelvis in transferring body weight from the vertebral column to the lower limbs.

Bone development

The adult hip bone is the result of three separate bones merging during late adolescence.

Key terms

  1. Coxal bone fusion — The process occurring in late teenage years where three bones join into one.

Ilium: The large, fan-shaped superior portion.

Ischium: The posteroinferior portion.

Pubis: The anteromedial portion.

Anterior articulation

The two hip bones are joined at the front of the body by the pubic symphysis, which is a specialized joint.

Key terms

  1. Pubic symphysis — The specialized joint where the pubic portions of the right and left hip bones join anteriorly.

Joint formation

The three components of the hip bone meet at a deep, cup-shaped cavity to receive the head of the femur.

Key terms

  1. Acetabulum — The deep cavity on the lateral hip bone where the ilium, ischium, and pubis converge.

Ligamentous support

Strong bands of connective tissue immobilize and support the sacrum within the pelvic girdle.

Key terms

  1. Sacrospinous ligament — A ligament spanning from the sacrum to the ischial spine.
  2. Sacrotuberous ligament — A ligament spanning from the sacrum to the ischial tuberosity.
  3. Anterior/Posterior sacroiliac ligaments — Pairs of strong ligaments that support and stabilize the sacroiliac joint.

Organ containment

The pelvic cavity is divided into superior and inferior regions based on the organs they house.

Key terms

  1. Greater pelvis — The superior region occupied by portions of the small and large intestines.
  2. Lesser pelvis — The inferior region containing the bladder and other pelvic organs.

Pelvic stability

Immobility of the pelvic girdle provides a steady foundation for the upper body.

Key terms

  1. Stability — The state of the pelvis being strongly united and immobile to enable effective body weight transfer.

Connective attachments

A fibrous membrane unites the two bones of the leg to provide a site for muscle attachment.

Key terms

  1. Interosseous membrane of the leg — A sheet of dense connective tissue that unites the tibia and fibula bones.

Patellar tracking

Imbalances in muscle pull can cause the kneecap to deviate from its proper path, leading to patellofemoral syndrome.

Key terms

  1. Abnormal tracking — The deviation of the patella toward the lateral side of the patellar surface on the distal femur.
  2. Q-angle — A measure of how far the femur is angled laterally away from the vertical line of the tibia.

Metatarsal structure

Five elongated bones form the mid-foot region between the tarsal bones and the phalanges.

Key terms

  1. Metatarsal bone — One of the five elongated bones forming the mid-foot group.

Ankle joint articulation

The ankle joint is formed by three specific points of contact between the leg bones and the foot.

Key terms

  1. Talus — The most superior tarsal bone that articulates with the tibia and fibula.
  2. Medial malleolus — The expansion on the medial distal tibia that articulates with the talus.
  3. Lateral malleolus — The distal end of the fibula that articulates with the lateral side of the talus.

Muscle leverage

Bony projections and sesamoid bones increase the efficiency of muscle pull across joints.

Key terms

  1. Patella function — The action of lifting the tendon away from the joint to increase the leverage power of the quadriceps.
  2. Greater trochanter — A large bony projection on the proximal femur that provides additional leverage for hip muscles.

Tibial landmarks

The shin bone features several prominent features that can be felt through the skin or serve as muscle attachment sites.

Key terms

  1. Tibial tuberosity — An elevated area on the anterior proximal tibia for the patellar muscle tendon attachment.
  2. Medial malleolus — The large bony bump found on the medial side of the ankle region.
  3. Anterior border — The anterior apex of the triangular tibial shaft that runs inferiorly from the tibial tuberosity.

Patellar function

As a sesamoid bone, the kneecap protects tendons from damage and increases muscle efficiency.

Key terms

  1. Sesamoid bone — A bone incorporated into a muscle tendon where it crosses a joint to prevent friction damage.
  2. Quadriceps femoris tendon — The structure in which the patella is embedded to facilitate knee movement.

Leg bone union

The tibia and fibula articulate with each other at both the knee and ankle regions.

Key terms

  1. Proximal tibiofibular joint — The articulation between the head of the fibula and the inferior aspect of the lateral tibial condyle.
  2. Distal tibiofibular joint — The articulation between the distal end of the fibula and the fibular notch of the tibia.

Femoral muscle attachment

The femur features several ridges and projections that provide broad or powerful attachment points for hip and thigh muscles.

Key terms

  1. Greater trochanter — A large projection above the femoral neck where multiple muscles that act across the hip joint attach.
  2. Lesser trochanter — A small prominence on the medial aspect of the femur for the attachment of a powerful muscle.
  3. Linea aspera — A roughened ridge passing distally along the posterior mid-femur for multiple muscle attachments.
  4. Gluteal tuberosity — A roughened area on the proximal posterior shaft extending inferiorly from the greater trochanter.

Femoral blood supply

A specific ligamentous pathway allows an artery to reach and nourish the head of the thigh bone.

Key terms

  1. Fovea capitis — A minor indentation on the medial side of the femoral head for ligament attachment.
  2. Ligament of the head of the femur — A weak ligament spanning the femur and acetabulum that carries an artery to the femoral head.

Weight bearing

Primary support of body weight is concentrated on the larger bone of the leg and specific bones of the foot.

Key terms

  1. Tibia — The larger, main weight-bearing bone of the lower leg.
  2. Talus — The tarsal bone that receives weight from the tibia and transfers it to the heel.
  3. Calcaneus — The heel bone that receives weight from the talus and rests on the ground.

Fibular function

Though it does not support body weight, the slender lateral leg bone is essential for muscle attachment.

Key terms

  1. Fibula — The thin, non-weight-bearing bone located on the lateral side of the leg.
