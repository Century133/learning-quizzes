Ch8 Exam · 8.1–8.4

  1. Inferior scapular angle

The inferior angle represents the lowest point of the triangular scapula and serves as a critical site for the attachment of major muscles.

Key terms

  1. Inferior angle of the scapula - The lowest corner of the bone, located where the medial and lateral borders meet.
  2. Muscle attachment point - A site for several powerful muscles involved in executing intensive movements of the shoulder and upper limb.
  3. Sternoclavicular joint

This joint provides the sole bony articulation between the pectoral girdle and the axial skeleton of the body.

Key terms

  1. Sternal end of the clavicle - The triangular medial end of the bone that articulates with the manubrium of the sternum.
  2. Sternoclavicular joint - The primary anchor point between the appendicular and axial skeletons for the upper limb.
    • Upward/downward and anterior/posterior - The specific directions of movement permitted by this joint during shoulder activity.
  3. Clavicle morphology

The clavicle is an S-shaped long bone that exhibits distinct physical variations based on biological sex and physical demand.

Key terms

  1. Clavicle - The only long bone in the body that lies in a horizontal position.
    • Female morphology - A version of the bone that tends to be shorter, thinner, and less curved.
    • Male morphology - A version of the bone that is heavier, longer, and possesses a greater curvature with rougher surfaces.
  2. Clavicle function

The clavicle acts as a mechanical strut that supports the scapula while facilitating force transmission and protecting deep neurovascular pathways.

Key terms

  1. Strut - A lateral extension that holds the shoulder joint away from the body trunk.
  2. Maximal freedom of motion - The lateral positioning provided by the clavicle that allows the upper limb a wide range of movement.
  3. Force transmission - The process of conducting physical stress and impact from the upper limb to the sternum and axial skeleton.
  4. Scapular tubercles

These small bony projections located around the glenoid cavity provide essential attachment points for muscles of the arm.

Key terms

  1. Supraglenoid tubercle - A small bony bump located immediately above the glenoid cavity.
  2. Infraglenoid tubercle - A small bony bump located immediately below the glenoid cavity.
  3. Vascular protection

The anatomical positioning of the clavicle serves as a shield for vital structures traveling from the trunk into the arm.

Key terms

  1. Protective positioning - The location of the clavicle overlying essential pathways to safeguard underlying structures.
  2. Neurovascular structures - The essential nerves and blood vessels that pass between the trunk of the body and the upper limb.
  3. Acromial end

The lateral extremity of the clavicle articulates with the scapula to form the bony tip of the superior shoulder region.

Key terms

  1. Acromial end of the clavicle - The lateral portion of the bone that articulates with the acromion of the scapula.
  2. Acromion - The flattened and expanded lateral projection of the scapular spine.
  3. Scapular borders

The margins of the triangular scapula are named based on their anatomical positions relative to the body's axes.

Key terms

  1. Superior border - The upper margin of the scapula, featuring the suprascapular notch.
  2. Medial border - The long margin of the scapula facing the vertebral column.
  3. Lateral border - The margin of the scapula facing the arm.
  4. Limb functional demands

The upper and lower limbs are differentiated by evolutionary adaptations for high mobility versus weight-bearing stability.

Key terms

  1. Upper limb adaptations - Highly mobile structures designed for a wide variety of activities and object manipulation.
  2. Lower limb adaptations - Robust structures adapted for weight-bearing support, stability, and locomotion.
  3. Costoclavicular ligament

This ligament provides indirect support to the medial pectoral girdle by anchoring the clavicle to the thoracic cage.

Key terms

  1. Costoclavicular ligament - A connective tissue band that spans the sternal end of the clavicle and the underlying first rib.
  2. First rib - The superior rib that serves as the anchor point for the costoclavicular ligament.
  3. Pectoral girdle mobility

The shoulder region achieves an extensive range of motion through the independent operation of the right and left skeletal girdles.

Key terms

  1. Pectoral girdle - The structure consisting of the scapula and clavicle that attaches the upper limb to the axial skeleton.
  2. Structural independence - The characteristic where the right and left girdles are not joined to each other, allowing separate operation.
  3. Hand gripping mechanics

Power gripping is enhanced by the anterior mobility of the medial metacarpal bones within the palm.

Key terms

  1. Metacarpal mobility - The limited anterior-posterior movement available to the medial bones of the hand.
  2. Fourth and fifth metacarpals - The specific bones of the palm that move anteriorly to increase the strength of contact during gripping.
  3. Muscle tendon pathways

Specialized bony grooves and ligamentous passages protect the tendons that cross the shoulder and wrist joints.

Key terms

  1. Intertubercular groove (bicipital groove) - A narrow sulcus on the humerus that provides passage for the biceps brachii tendon.
  2. Carpal tunnel - A narrow passageway at the base of the hand for muscle tendons and a major nerve.
  3. Wrist joint movement

Movement of the hand relative to the forearm is facilitated by a combination of two distinct joint levels.

Key terms

  1. Radiocarpal joint - The articulation between the distal radius and specific proximal carpal bones.
  2. Midcarpal joint - The articulation between the proximal and distal rows of carpal bones.
  3. Upper limb fractures

Fractures of the upper limb often involve the clavicle, humerus, or radius and are frequently caused by falls onto an outstretched hand.

Key terms

  1. Clavicular fracture - A break often occurring between the middle and lateral portions of the bone due to falls or blows.
  2. Surgical neck fracture - A break occurring at the base of the proximal humerus, common in arm injuries.
  3. Scaphoid fracture - A common carpal fracture that is slow to heal with a danger of bone necrosis due to poor blood supply.

Names and studies

  1. Colles fracture - A complete transverse fracture of the distal radius occurring from a fall on an outstretched hand that results in a "dinner fork" bend.
  2. Elbow joint mechanics

The elbow joint is formed by the articulation of the distal humerus with the radius and ulna, accommodated by specific fossae.

Key terms

  1. Trochlea - A spindle-shaped region of the distal humerus that articulates with the ulna.
  2. Capitulum - A knob-like structure on the anterior distal humerus that articulates with the radius.
  3. Olecranon fossa - A posterior depression on the humerus that receives the olecranon process of the ulna during arm extension.
  4. Forearm joint articulation

The radius and ulna connect at two points to allow for rotational movements of the forearm.

Key terms

  1. Proximal radioulnar joint - The articulation between the head of the radius and the radial notch of the ulna.
  2. Distal radioulnar joint - The articulation between the head of the ulna and the ulnar notch of the radius.
  3. Proximal humerus features

The upper end of the humerus contains the head for shoulder articulation and tubercles for muscle insertion.

Key terms

  1. Head of the humerus - The smooth, medially facing region that articulates with the glenoid cavity.
  2. Greater tubercle - An expanded bony area on the lateral side of the proximal humerus.
  3. Lesser tubercle - A bony area found on the anterior aspect of the proximal humerus.
  4. Carpal tunnel function

This wrist passageway allows tendons and a major nerve to enter the hand while being protected by a ligamentous roof.

Key terms

  1. Flexor retinaculum - A strong ligament that forms the roof of the carpal tunnel and maintains the grouping of carpal bones.
  2. Carpal tunnel contents - The nine muscle tendons and one major nerve that pass through the wrist into the hand.
  3. Muscle attachment sites

Roughened regions known as tuberosities are located on the long bones to serve as specific points for muscle insertion.

Key terms

  1. Deltoid tuberosity - A V-shaped region on the lateral humerus shaft for the attachment of the deltoid muscle.
  2. Ulnar tuberosity - A roughened area on the anterior ulna just below the coronoid process.
  3. Radial tuberosity - An oval-shaped bony protuberance on the medial radius for muscle attachment.
  4. Thumb mobility

The unique positioning and separation of the first metacarpal bone provide the thumb with its essential range of motion.

Key terms

  1. First metacarpal bone - The bone at the base of the thumb that is separated from the other metacarpals.
  2. Freedom of motion - The independent mobility that allows the thumb to move separately for object manipulation.
  3. Pelvic boundaries

The internal space of the pelvis is divided into two distinct regions by a skeletal line called the pelvic brim.

Key terms

  1. Pelvic brim (pelvic inlet) - The superior margin that separates the greater pelvis from the lesser pelvis.
  2. Pelvic outlet - The inferior opening defined by the inferior margins of the pubic symphysis, ischiopubic rami, and the coccyx.
  3. Ligamentous support

A group of strong ligaments secures the sacrum to the hip bones to immobilize the pelvis for weight-bearing.

Key terms

  1. Anterior and posterior sacroiliac ligaments - Strong connective bands that support the joint between the sacrum and the ilium.
  2. Sacrospinous ligament - A ligament extending from the sacrum to the ischial spine.
  3. Sacrotuberous ligament - A ligament extending from the sacrum to the ischial tuberosity.
  4. Joint formation

Lower limb joints are formed by the articulation of the hip bones, femur, and tibia to support movement and weight.

Key terms

  1. Hip joint - The articulation between the head of the femur and the acetabulum of the hip bone.
  2. Knee joint - The articulation between the femoral condyles and the tibial condyles.
  3. Bony landmarks

Several features of the hip bone are palpable through the skin and serve as vital anatomical reference points.

Key terms

  1. Iliac crest - The curved superior margin of the ilium felt along the waistline.
  2. Anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) - A rounded bony landmark felt at the anterolateral hip.
  3. Ischial tuberosity - A large roughened area of the inferior ischium that supports body weight when sitting.
  4. Connective attachments

Dense connective tissue sheets unite parallel bones in the forearm and leg to provide structural integrity.

Key terms

  1. Interosseous membrane of the forearm - A fibrous sheet that unites the ulna and radius bones.
  2. Interosseous membrane of the leg - A fibrous sheet that unites the tibia and fibula bones.
  3. Nerve pathways

Specific openings in the posterior pelvis allow for the exit of neurovascular structures to the lower limb.

Key terms

  1. Greater sciatic foramen - The superior opening formed by the greater sciatic notch and sacrospinous ligament.
  2. Lesser sciatic foramen - The inferior opening formed by the lesser sciatic notch, sacrospinous, and sacrotuberous ligaments.
  3. Pelvic orientation

In the anatomical position, the pelvis is tilted forward so that specific landmarks align on a vertical plane.

Key terms

  1. Anterior tilt - The forward-leaning orientation of the pelvis when standing.
  2. Vertical plane alignment - The position where the anterior superior iliac spines and pubic tubercles are vertically aligned.
  3. Obstetric adaptation

The female pelvis features several structural modifications to provide a wider path for childbirth.

Key terms

  1. Pelvic width - The increased distance between the anterior superior iliac spines in females compared to males.
  2. Subpubic angle - The inverted V-shape greater than 80 degrees in females formed where the ischiopubic rami meet.
  3. Pelvic inlet shape - The rounded or oval shape of the pelvic brim found in females.
  4. Weight distribution

The rigid structure of the pelvis is designed to transfer the weight of the upper body to the lower limbs.

Key terms

  1. Weight transfer - The process of moving body weight from the vertebral column through the pelvic girdle to the hip joints.
  2. Stability - The strong foundation for the upper body provided by the largely immobile pelvic structure.
  3. Sex determination

Forensic analysis relies heavily on the hip bone because it provides the most accurate evidence for determining biological sex.

Key terms

  1. Hip bone - The most reliable bone in the human body for accurate sex determination.
  2. Anterior articulation

The right and left hip bones join anteriorly to form a joint and a structural arch.

Key terms

  1. Pubic symphysis - The specialized joint where the two pubic bones meet at the front of the pelvis.
  2. Pubic arch - The bony structure formed by the pubic symphysis and the adjacent pubic rami.
  3. Organ containment

The pelvis is divided into sub-cavities that house different sets of abdominal and pelvic organs.

Key terms

  1. Greater pelvis - The superior region occupied by portions of the small and large intestines.
  2. Lesser pelvis - The inferior space containing the bladder and other internal pelvic organs.
  3. Ankle joint articulation

The ankle joint is formed by the union of the talus with the distal projections of the tibia and fibula.

Key terms

  1. Talus - The superior-most tarsal bone that articulates with the leg bones.
  2. Medial malleolus - The bony bump on the medial side of the ankle formed by the tibia.
  3. Lateral malleolus - The bony bump on the lateral side of the ankle formed by the fibula.
  4. Muscle leverage

Certain bony projections and sesamoid bones serve to increase the mechanical advantage of the muscles acting on joints.

Key terms

  1. Greater trochanter - A large bony projection on the femur that gives additional leverage to hip muscles.
  2. Patella - The kneecap, which lifts the quadriceps tendon away from the joint to increase leverage.
  3. Metatarsal structure

The mid-foot is composed of five elongated bones that connect the tarsals to the digits.

Key terms

  1. Metatarsal bones - Five bones numbered 1–5 starting from the medial side of the foot.
  2. Patellar tracking

The patella must move correctly within the femoral groove to avoid pain and joint damage.

Key terms

  1. Lateral pull - The superior and lateral force exerted on the patella by the quadriceps femoris muscle.
  2. Abnormal tracking - A condition where the patella deviates too far laterally, potentially leading to patellofemoral syndrome.
  3. Tibial landmarks

The tibia features expanded condyles for the knee joint and an anterior ridge that is easily felt through the skin.

Key terms

  1. Medial and lateral condyles of the tibia - The expanded proximal surfaces that articulate with the femur.
  2. Tibial tuberosity - The elevated anterior area that serves as the attachment point for the patellar tendon.
  3. Anterior border - The palpable anterior apex of the triangular tibial shaft.
  4. Pelvic width influence

The width of the pelvis determines the diagonal angle of the femur relative to the vertical tibia.

Key terms

  1. Q-angle - A measure of the lateral deviation of the femur from the vertical tibia, normally 10–15 degrees.
    • Female variation - A larger angle due to the increased width of the female pelvis.
    • Male variation - A smaller angle resulting from the narrower pelvic structure in males.
  2. Femoral blood supply

A minor indentation on the femoral head serves as an attachment for a ligament carrying an essential artery.

Key terms

  1. Fovea capitis - A minor indentation on the medial side of the femoral head.
  2. Ligament of the head of the femur - A ligament that carries an important artery supplying the head of the femur.
  3. Tarsal support

The heel bone possesses a specialized shelf-like projection to support the medial aspect of the ankle.

Key terms

  1. Calcaneus - The largest bone of the foot, commonly known as the heel bone.
  2. Sustentaculum tali - A prominent bony extension on the medial calcaneus that supports the talus bone.
  3. Weight bearing

Body weight is primarily transferred from the leg through the medial shin bone to the heel.

Key terms

  1. Tibia - The main weight-bearing bone of the lower leg.
  2. Calcaneus - The foot bone that receives body weight from the talus and rests on the ground.
  3. Leg bone union

The distal ends of the tibia and fibula articulate to stabilize the lateral side of the ankle region.

Key terms

  1. Distal tibiofibular joint - The articulation between the distal ends of the tibia and fibula.
  2. Fibular notch - A wide groove on the lateral side of the distal tibia that receives the fibula.
  3. Phalanx count

The digits of the hands and feet contain a consistent total of 14 small bones arranged in proximal, middle, and distal rows.

Key terms

  1. Phalanges - The individual bones that make up the fingers or toes.
    • Pollex/Hallux - The first digit (thumb or big toe) which contains only 2 phalanx bones.
    • Digits 2–5 - The remaining four digits which each contain 3 phalanx bones.
