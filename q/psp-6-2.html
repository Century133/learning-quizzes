6.2 Inferring Dispositions Using Causal Attribution

Role Expectations

Personality inferences are significantly easier to make when we observe behaviors that either violate established social norms or contradict the specific requirements of a social role.

Key Terms

  • Causal attribution: The social-cognitive process of attempting to determine the underlying causes of an individual's behavior.
  • Personal (internal or dispositional) attribution: The inference that a behavior was caused primarily by the person’s internal traits or characteristics rather than by the environment.
    • Example: If Tess sticks her tongue out at you instead of shaking your hand, the violation of social expectations makes it easier to infer she is an unfriendly person.

Names and Studies

Jones, Davis, & Gergen (1961)

  • Method: Participants viewed one of four videos of a job applicant. The role was either for a submariner (requiring an extrovert) or an astronaut (requiring an introvert). The applicant then acted either extroverted or introverted.
  • Finding: When the applicant's behavior violated role expectations (e.g., an extroverted astronaut candidate), participants made much stronger personality attributions. Specifically, participants believed the applicant's extraverted responses represented their true personality 91% of the time when applying for the introvert-coded astronaut role, compared to only 45% of the time when the same responses were expected for the submariner role.

Covariation Principle

The covariation principle suggests that we determine if a behavior is situational by observing how that behavior changes or "covaries" across different social contexts and time.

Key Terms

  • Consistency information: Determining whether a specific situation always produces the same behavior from the individual (e.g., a person cries at every wedding they attend).
  • Distinctiveness information: Determining whether the behavior occurs only in a specific situation and nowhere else (e.g., a person who only cries at weddings but remains composed in all other emotional settings).
  • Consensus information: Determining whether most people behave in the same way when faced with that specific situation (e.g., observing that many other guests are also crying at the wedding).

Weiner’s Model

This model categorizes the perceived causes of success and failure based on two primary dimensions: Locus (Internal vs. External) and Stability (Stable vs. Unstable).

Key Terms

  • Locus: The determination of whether the cause of an event is internal to the person or external to the situation.
    • Personal causes: Internal factors, such as ability and motivation.
  • Stability: The determination of whether a cause is likely to stay the same over time or change.
    • Situational causes: External factors, such as luck and task difficulty.

The 2x2 Attribution Framework

  • Ability [Internal / Stable]: An internal cause of success perceived as a fixed trait (e.g., natural intelligence).
  • Motivation [Internal / Unstable]: An internal cause of success that can fluctuate (e.g., working hard for one specific exam).
  • Task Difficulty [External / Stable]: A situational cause that remains constant (e.g., a consistently easy or hard course).
  • Luck [External / Unstable]: A situational cause that is unpredictable and subject to change (e.g., guessing correctly on a multiple-choice test).

Fundamental Attribution Error

The fundamental attribution error is the pervasive human tendency to overestimate the influence of personal factors while simultaneously underestimating the impact of the social situation when judging others.

Key Terms

  • Fundamental attribution error (correspondence bias): The systematic bias of overemphasizing internal person factors when explaining the behavior of others.
    • Example: Assuming a diner who leaves a large tip is naturally generous, without considering they may be using the tip to impress their companions.

Names and Studies

Skitka, Mullen, Griffin, Hutchinson, & Chamberlin (2002)

  • Method: In a trivia game study, participants were randomly assigned to be "quizmasters" or "contestants." Quizmasters were instructed to generate difficult questions based on their own idiosyncratic or esoteric knowledge.
  • Result: Despite knowing the roles were randomly assigned, observers rated the quizmasters as significantly more intelligent than the contestants, failing to account for the massive situational advantage provided by the quizmasters' ability to choose the topics.

Actor-Observer Difference

The actor-observer difference explains why individuals tend to attribute their own behaviors to situational requirements while attributing the exact same behaviors in others to stable internal traits.

Key Terms

  • Actor-observer difference: The tendency to make more situational attributions for oneself than for others.
    • Example: Describing oneself as "shy depending on the situation" (situational), while labeling a friend as simply "shy" (personal trait).

Names and Studies

Nisbett, Caputo, Legant, & Marecek (1973)

  • Method: College students were asked to choose trait terms (e.g., "energetic," "relaxed") to describe themselves, their best friend, their father, and the newscaster Walter Cronkite.
  • Result: Participants selected the "depends on the situation" option significantly more often for themselves than they did for any of the other three people, demonstrating a bias toward situational explanations for their own behavior.

Attributional Accuracy

While human attributional skills are generally "good enough" for social navigation, the process is highly prone to spontaneous biases, particularly when cognitive resources are low.

Key Terms

  • Salience: The degree to which a person stands out as the focus of attention in an environment.
    • Effect: Because we are "directly watching" the person, our visual orientation makes them seem like the primary cause of an action. When we act, we look outward at the situation; when we observe others, we look at the person, making their traits more salient than their context.
  • Spontaneous processing: The effortless, automatic, and rapid nature of making personal attributions without conscious deliberation.
  • Cognitive load: A state of being tired, distracted, or busy. This increases attribution errors because the "adjustment" phase—where we try to account for the situation—requires effortful thought and is frequently insufficient or skipped entirely.

Self-Serving Attributions

Individuals frequently distort their causal attributions to protect their self-esteem or maintain a positive self-image.

Key Terms

  • Self-serving attributions: Attributions designed to meet the desire to see oneself in a positive light.
    • Example: A teacher taking personal credit for a class's high test scores ("I am a great teacher") while blaming the students' lack of effort for poor scores.

Choice and Attribution

Personal attributions are far more likely to be made when an observer perceives that the actor chose their behavior freely without external coercion.

Key Terms

  • Situational attribution: The decision that a behavior was caused primarily by environmental pressures or external threats.
    • Example: Handing over a wallet to a gunman is attributed to the situation (the threat) rather than a person's inherent "generosity."

Names and Studies

Jones & Harris (1967)

  • Method: Participants read student essays (regarding political topics like Fidel Castro) and were told the authors either chose their own stance or were assigned a topic by a professor.
  • Result: Participants attributed the essay's viewpoints to the authors' true beliefs when they believed the author had a choice. However, even when told the topic was assigned, many participants still committed the fundamental attribution error by assuming the author's real beliefs matched the essay.
