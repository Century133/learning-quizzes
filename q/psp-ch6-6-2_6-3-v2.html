Heider's Causal Attribution and Person Perception

This foundational theory examines the cognitive processes people use to determine the underlying causes of observed behavior, acting as "amateur scientists" who collect data to infer hidden traits.

Key terms

  1. Person perception — The process of learning about people and their underlying traits by observing and interpreting their outward behaviors through the lens of existing mental schemas.
  2. Causal attribution — The social-cognitive process of attempting to determine whether a behavior was caused primarily by the person (internal) or the environment (external).

Names and studies

  1. Heider (1958) — Proposed that we are naturally curious about the "why" behind behavior and use observations to draw conclusions about a person’s stable personality traits.

Personal vs. Situational Attribution

Perceivers must distinguish between causes residing within the individual and those residing in the social context.

Key terms

  1. Personal attribution — The inference that a behavior was caused primarily by the person’s internal traits, characteristics, or disposition (e.g., "She left a big tip because she is generous").
  2. Situational attribution — The inference that a behavior was caused primarily by environmental pressures or external factors (e.g., "She left a big tip to impress her date").

Role Expectations

Personality inferences are significantly easier to make when we observe behaviors that either violate established social norms or contradict the requirements of a specific social role.

Key terms

  1. Role-consistent behavior — Actions that match the expected requirements of a social position, making the actor's true internal traits difficult to discern.
  2. Role-violating behavior — Actions that contradict social requirements, allowing observers to make strong dispositional (personal) inferences.

Names and studies

  1. Jones, Davis, & Gergen (1961) — Participants viewed job applicants for a submariner role (extrovert required) or astronaut role (introvert required). When the applicant acted contrary to the role (e.g., an extroverted astronaut candidate), participants made much stronger personality attributions.
    • Data Point: Participants believed extroverted responses reflected true personality 91% of the time when the job required introversion (astronaut), but only 45% of the time when extroversion was expected (submariner).

Choice and Attribution

Personal attributions are far more likely to be made when an observer perceives that the actor chose their behavior freely without external coercion.

Key terms

  1. Free choice — The perception that an actor acted without external pressure, strengthening the link between behavior and personality.
    • Example: We infer a person is generous only if they give money voluntarily, not if they are handing over a wallet while being threatened by a gunman.

Names and studies

  1. Jones & Harris (1967) — Participants read student essays about controversial political topics (e.g., Fidel Castro). Even when participants were told the authors were assigned the topic by a professor, they still committed the fundamental attribution error by assuming the authors' real beliefs matched the essay, though the effect was stronger when the topic was freely chosen.

Covariation Principle

The covariation principle suggests that we determine if a behavior is situational or personal by observing how that behavior changes or "covaries" across different people, times, and social contexts.

Key terms

  1. Consistency information — Does this situation always produce the same behavior from this individual?
  2. Distinctiveness information — Does the behavior occur only in this specific situation and nowhere else?
  3. Consensus information — Do most other people behave in the same way in this specific situation?

Interaction Mechanics

  • Situational Attribution: More likely when Consensus is High (everyone does it), Distinctiveness is High (the person only does it here), and Consistency is High (the person always does it here).
  • Personal Attribution: More likely when Consensus is Low (others don't do it), Distinctiveness is Low (the person does this everywhere), and Consistency is High.

Weiner’s 2x2 Model of Success and Failure

This model categorizes the perceived causes of outcomes based on two primary dimensions: Locus (Internal vs. External) and Stability (Stable vs. Unstable).

The Attribution Framework

  1. Ability [Internal / Stable] — Perceived as a fixed trait (e.g., "I am naturally smart").
  2. Motivation/Effort [Internal / Unstable] — Can fluctuate over time (e.g., "I studied hard for this specific test").
  3. Task Difficulty [External / Stable] — A situational factor that remains constant (e.g., "The math exam is always hard").
  4. Luck [External / Unstable] — An unpredictable situational cause (e.g., "I guessed correctly on the multiple-choice questions").

Names and studies

  1. Weiner (1985) — Established that how we attribute success and failure influences our future expectations and emotional reactions.

Fundamental Attribution Error

The fundamental attribution error (or correspondence bias) is the pervasive tendency to overestimate the influence of personal traits while underestimating the impact of the social situation when judging others.

Names and studies

  1. Skitka et al. (2002) — In a trivia study, quizmasters generated questions based on their own idiosyncratic or esoteric knowledge. Despite knowing roles were randomly assigned, observers rated quizmasters as more intelligent than contestants, failing to account for the situational advantage of choosing the questions.

Actor-Observer Difference

There is a consistent divergence in how individuals explain their own behaviors compared to how they explain the behaviors of others.

Key terms

  1. Actor-Observer Difference — The tendency to attribute our own actions to the situation while attributing others' identical actions to their stable internal traits.
  2. Synthesis Note: This is an expansion of the Fundamental Attribution Error. While the FAE focuses on our bias in judging others, the actor-observer difference highlights the asymmetry—we grant ourselves the situational grace we deny to others.

Names and studies

  1. Nisbett et al. (1973) — Found students used "depends on the situation" for themselves significantly more often than for their friends, fathers, or even famous figures like Walter Cronkite.

Salience, Spontaneous Processing, and Cognitive Load

Attributional errors are often driven by what we focus on and how much mental energy we have.

Key terms

  1. Salience — The degree to which a person stands out. We attribute more causality to whoever is the focus of our attention.
  2. Spontaneous trait inference — The effortless, automatic conclusion about personality that "pops" into mind before situational factors are considered.
  3. Cognitive Load — A state of being tired, distracted, or busy. Under high cognitive load, we are more likely to commit attribution errors because we lack the resources to "correct" our spontaneous personal attributions with situational logic.

Names and studies

  1. Taylor & Fiske (1975) — Demonstrated that people attribute more influence to the person they are directly watching in a social scene.
  2. Newman & Uleman (1989) — Established that personal attributions occur rapidly and without conscious deliberation.

Attributional Accuracy and Cognitive Accessibility

While our perceptions are "good enough" for social navigation, they are heavily influenced by the perceiver's internal state.

Key terms

  1. Cognitive accessibility — The ease with which a specific trait comes to mind.
    • Example: A fashion-focused person will notice clothing first; an athlete will notice physique first.
  2. Perceiver Overlap — The phenomenon where descriptions reflect the perceiver more than the target.

Names and studies

  1. Fiske (2003) — Argued that while not perfect, our attribution skills are sufficient for effective social interaction.
  2. Dornbusch et al. (1965) — Showed that descriptions provided by one perceiver about different people overlap more than descriptions of one target provided by different perceivers.

Need for Cognition

Individual differences in the motivation to engage in deep, effortful information processing influence the quality of attributions.

Key terms

  1. Need for cognition — The tendency to think carefully and fully about social situations.
    • High Need: Process information thoughtfully; make more causal attributions overall.
    • Low Need: Impulsive, impatient; make attributions more quickly and spontaneously.

Names and studies

  1. Cacioppo & Petty (1982) — Defined the drive to engage in and enjoy thoughtful information processing.
  2. Sargent (2004) — Found that people with a lower need for cognition reach attributional conclusions more quickly and punitively.
  3. Fletcher et al. (1986) — Discovered that psychology majors show significantly more curiosity about people than natural science majors.

Implicit Personality Theories

The underlying beliefs we hold about whether human traits are fixed or malleable significantly influence how we judge ourselves and others.

Key terms

  1. Entity theorists — Believe traits are stable and incapable of change. They focus on traits and make rapid personal attributions.
  2. Incremental theorists — Believe personality is dynamic and can change over time. They focus on situational factors and psychological processes.
    • Example: An incremental theorist sees a bad grade as a sign to study differently (changeable), whereas an entity theorist sees it as a sign of low intelligence (fixed).

Names and studies

  1. Blackwell et al. (2007) — Found that students with incremental theories of intelligence significantly improved their math grades over two years compared to entity theorists.
  2. Molden, Plaks, & Dweck (2006) — Found that under time pressure, incremental theorists prioritized situational cues, while entity theorists remained focused on personal traits.

Cultural Attribution Differences

Culture shifts the primary focus of attributions between the individual and their social context.

Key terms

  1. Individualistic (Western) Cultures — Prioritize personal responsibility and internal traits (e.g., U.S., Canada).
  2. Collectivistic (East Asian) Cultures — Prioritize situational context and social relationships (e.g., Japan, China, India).

Names and studies

  1. Miller (1984) — Found that attributional differences are learned; young children (8–11) are similar, but by adulthood, Americans make more personal attributions while Indians make more situational ones.
  2. Hong et al. (2000) — Showed that bicultural individuals shift mindsets based on cues. Priming Hong Kong students with American icons (U.S. flag) led to more personal attributions for a boy's behavior.
  3. Masuda & Nisbett (2001) — Found Japanese students remembered background/contextual objects (frogs, plants) more than American students, who focused on salient foreground objects (fish).

Attributional Mental Health and Learned Helplessness

Habitual attribution styles determine psychological resilience and vulnerability to depression.

Key terms

  1. Negative Attributional Style — Explaining negative events through Internal, Stable, and Global qualities of the self (e.g., "I failed because I am no good [internal], I always fail [stable], I fail at everything [global]").
  2. Positive Attributional Style — Explaining negative events through External, Unstable, and Specific qualities (e.g., "The test was hard [external], I'll do better next time [unstable], I'm still good at other subjects [specific]").
  3. Learned Helplessness — A state of passivity occurring when people make External, Stable, and Global attributions for their behavior, leading to a perceived loss of control (e.g., "Nothing I do matters; the environment is rigged against me").

Names and studies

  1. Alloy et al. (1999) — Found students with negative attributional styles were significantly more likely to experience clinical depression.
  2. Abramson, Seligman, & Teasdale (1978) — Demonstrated that inescapable negative events (like noise or shocks) lead to a lack of effort even when escape becomes possible.

Self-Handicapping Strategies

Individuals proactively create external excuses for potential failure to protect self-esteem from personal (ability) blame.

Key terms

  1. Self-handicapping — Engaging in behaviors that create a convenient external attribution for a future failure (e.g., partying before a big exam).

Gender Differences and Studies

  1. Berglas & Jones (1978) — Found that men are more likely to engage in behavioral self-handicapping, such as choosing performance-inhibiting drugs to provide an excuse for poor test performance.
  2. Hirt, Deppe, & Gordon (1991) — Found that women are more likely to self-handicap through verbal claims, such as citing stress or time constraints as reasons for potential failure.

Optimism vs. Realism

Mental health requires a balance between a positive outlook and an accurate assessment of risk.

Key terms

  1. Unrealistic optimism — The tendency to be overly positive about avoiding negative events (e.g., believing one is immune to the risks of drunk driving). This can lead to dangerous, risky behavior.

Names and studies

  1. Taylor & Armor (1996) — Found that most healthy people maintain a functional balance between optimism and realistic goal setting, helping them persist without ignoring reality.
