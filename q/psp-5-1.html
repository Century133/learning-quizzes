Genetic transmission

Attitudes are partially passed from parents to offspring through genetic inheritance via genetic transmission.

Key terms

  1. Heritability — Extent genetic variation determines specific attitudes.
    1. ex. High heritability for abortion (0.54) and roller coasters (0.52) vs. zero (0) for playing bingo.

Names and studies

  1. Olson, Vernon, Harris, & Jang (2001) — Used twin studies to rank heritability; found inherited attitudes form earlier and are more resistant to change.

Attitude strength

Strong attitudes are important, frequently used evaluations characterized by high cognitive accessibility.

Key terms

  1. Attitude strength — Importance of an attitude; assessed by how quickly it comes to mind.
  2. Cognitive accessibility — Ease and speed of attitude activation.
    1. ex. Stating a political preference quickly indicates high accessibility.

Names and studies

  1. Bargh, Chaiken, Raymond, & Hymes (1996) — Found people form weak, low-impact attitudes toward nonsense words like "juvalamu."
  2. Fazio, Powell, & Herr (1983) — Compared direct puzzle work vs. observation; direct experience significantly decreased reaction time (faster response).

Attitude consistency

Attitude consistency occurs when emotional, behavioral, and knowledge-based components of an evaluation align for an object.

Key terms

  1. Principle of attitude consistency — Theory that ABC components normally align for any given attitude object.
  2. ABC components — Three fundamental parts of an attitude.
    1. Affect — Feelings or emotions toward object.
    2. Behavior — Actions taken in relation to object.
    3. Cognition — Beliefs or knowledge about object.

Names and studies

  1. Glasman & Albarracín (2006) — Meta-analysis showing significant positive correlations between attitude components and behavior.

Introspection effects

Analyzing the specific reasons behind an evaluation can decrease its validity by disrupting the influence of affective components.

Key terms

  1. Introspection — Act of thinking about reasons for one's attitudes.
    1. ex. Listing features of a jam rather than relying on pure taste preference.

Names and studies

  1. Wilson & Schooler (1991) — Found listing reasons for liking jams reduced correlation between student and expert ratings.
  2. MacDonald, Zanna, & Fong (1996) — Found alcohol makes affect dominate cognition, increasing intentions to have unprotected sex.

Attitude-behavior consistency

Attitudes accurately predict actions only when situational conditions, self-awareness, and measurement levels align.

Key terms

  1. Attitude-behavior relationship — Correlation between an evaluation and resulting action.
  2. Self-awareness — State of focusing on self-concept; increases attitude-behavior alignment.
    1. ex. Standing in front of a mirror or TV camera.

Names and studies

  1. Wicker (1969) — Identified that attitude-behavior relationship is not universal; depends on situation and person.
  2. Beaman, Klentz, Diener, & Svanum (1979) — Used mirrors to activate self-concept; increased likelihood of participants acting on internal attitudes.

Measurement specificity

Predictive accuracy improves when the level of detail in an attitude question matches the detail of the behavior studied.

Key terms

  1. Measurement specificity — Practice of assessing attitudes at level matching specific behavior being predicted.
    1. ex. Asking about "birth control use in next 2 years" rather than general "birth control."

Names and studies

  1. Davidson & Jaccard (1979) — Measured birth control attitudes at varying levels; specific questions best predicted actual use.

Self-monitoring

Individual differences in the tendency to prioritize social cues over internal attitudes dictate behavioral consistency.

Key terms

  1. Self-monitoring — Tendency to attend to social cues and adjust behavior to environment.
  2. High self-monitors — Individuals blending into social situations to be liked; show low attitude-behavior consistency.
  3. Low self-monitors — Individuals less concerned with social norms; more likely to act on internal attitudes.

Names and studies

  1. Kraus (1995) — Meta-analysis confirming relationship between attitudes and behavior is weaker for high self-monitors.

Neural basis

Attitudes are stored in specific brain regions and can be activated within a fraction of a second.

Key terms

  1. Prefrontal cortex — Brain region where social knowledge and attitudes primarily stored.
  2. Amygdala — Region critical for emotional attitudes, particularly fear-based evaluations.

Names and studies

  1. Cunningham, Raye, & Johnson (2004) — Used neuroimaging to find different regions for cognitive vs. emotional evaluations.
  2. Handy, Smilek, Geiger, Liu, & Schooler (2010) — Provided ERP evidence that attitudes activate within one-fifth of a second.

Evolutionary functions

Maintaining evaluations provides survival value by enabling rapid, effortless decisions to approach or avoid stimuli.

Key terms

  1. Functional attitudes — Evaluations allowing rapid decisions regarding survival.
    1. ex. "Snake = bad" (avoidance) vs. "blueberries = good" (approach).

Names and studies

  1. Duckworth, Bargh, Garcia, & Chaiken (2002) — Argued attitudes provide substantial value by allowing quick approach or avoidance decisions.

Theory of planned behavior

Intentional actions are best predicted by the combination of strong intentions, social situations, and attitude strength.

Key terms

  1. Theory of planned behavior — Model identifying intentions, social situations/norms, and attitude strength as behavioral predictors.
  2. Intentions — Internal resolve to perform behavior; serves as primary predictor of action.

Names and studies

  1. Fishbein & Ajzen (1975) — Developed theory stating strong intentions are the necessary link between attitudes and difficult behaviors.

Definition of attitude

Social psychology defines attitude as a relatively enduring evaluation that connects the self-concept to an object.

Key terms

  1. Attitude — Relatively enduring evaluation of something.
  2. Attitude object — Specific person, product, or social group being evaluated.
    1. ex. Brand of cereal, political figure, or social group.

Names and studies

  1. Albarracín, Johnson, & Zanna (2005) — Defined parameters of attitude object and nature of enduring evaluations.
