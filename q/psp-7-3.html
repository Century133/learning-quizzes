7.3 Person, Gender, and Cultural Differences in Conformity

Person differences

Conformity represents an interaction between the situation and the person, though individual traits typically exert less influence than situational factors.

Key terms

  1. Optimal balance (Brewer, 2003) — the desire to maintain a specific equilibrium between being similar to and different from others.
    1. ex. expressing individuality when feeling too similar to a group to restore distinctiveness.
  2. Self-esteem — self-worth levels that influence the need for belonging; lower levels correlate with higher conformity for acceptance.
  3. Group identification — the degree to which an individual identifies with a specific group, increasing adherence to that group’s norms.
  4. Need for uniqueness (Snyder & Fromkin, 1977) — a personality variable driving individuals to resist conformity to maintain identity.

Names and studies

  1. Solomon Asch — line-judging research where 24% of participants never conformed despite strong situational pressure from the majority.
  2. Visser & Krosnick (1998) — found that susceptibility to influence is lowest for individuals in their 40s and 50s.

Gender differences

Small gender differences in conformity reflect an interaction between gender and social context, appearing primarily in public settings.

Key terms

  1. Self-concern — a motivation more common in men to appear high-status and independent from the opinions of others.
  2. Other-concern — a motivation more common in women to connect with others and maintain group harmony.
  3. Public vs. Private setting — the situational distinction where gender differences manifest publicly but largely disappear in private.
  4. Double-bind — the conflict women face between masculine leadership requirements and traditional feminine social role expectations.
    1. ex. being disliked for acting assertively or "masculine" to gain power while being required to do so for success.
  5. Transformational leadership style — an interpersonally oriented approach allowing women to lead effectively without being perceived as excessively masculine.

Names and studies

  1. Milgram — conducted obedience studies that found no significant differences in conformity rates between men and women.
  2. Griskevicius et al. (2006) — showed men primed with romantic attraction were less likely to conform to demonstrate status and mate quality.
  3. Nyquist & Spence (1986) — found dominant men became leaders 90% of the time, while dominant women did so only 35% of the time.
  4. Eagly, Karau, & Makhijani (1995) — performed a meta-analysis showing no overall difference in leadership effectiveness between genders.
  5. Fiske et al. (1991) — documented a Supreme Court case where a female top-performer was denied promotion for acting too "masculine."

Cultural differences

Cultural orientation significantly impacts conformity, with collectivist societies prioritizing group harmony over the individualistic value of uniqueness.

Key terms

  1. Individualistic culture — a society that prioritizes independence and personal uniqueness over group adherence.
  2. Collectivistic culture — a society that emphasizes harmony, group goals, and conformity to social norms.

Names and studies

  1. Bond & Smith (1996) — meta-analysis of 133 Asch-style studies across 17 countries finding higher conformity in collectivist cultures.
  2. Kim & Markus (1999) — found U.S. ads focus on uniqueness ("Individualize") while Korean ads focus on conformity ("Harmonious society").
  3. Figure 7.10 ([SOURCE_IMAGE_1]) — data showing conformity themes in approximately 95% of Korean magazine ads versus 65% of U.S. ads.

Psychological reactance

Psychological reactance occurs when perceived threats to personal freedom trigger a refusal to conform or a move in the opposite direction.

Key terms

  1. Psychological reactance (Brehm, 1966) — a strong motivational state resulting from threatened or eliminated freedom of choice.
    1. ex. a child refusing to eat vegetables specifically because they are being forced to do so.

Names and studies

  1. Pennebaker & Sanders (1976) — found more graffiti in restrooms with "Do not write" signs than those with "Please don't" signs.
  2. Wolf & Montgomery (1977) — showed jurors were more likely to use inadmissible evidence when a judge strictly ordered them to ignore it.
  3. Bushman & Stack (1996) — found that warning labels on violent films increased participant interest due to threatened freedom of choice.
  4. Kray et al. (2004) — demonstrated "Stereotype Reactance" where telling women they were poor negotiators led them to work harder and succeed.
  5. Shoham, Trost, & Rohrbaugh (2004) — observed patients sought substance abuse treatment more often once family members stopped pressuring them.

Informational influence

The desire for accurate knowledge drives conformity, particularly when individuals lack expertise in a specific domain.

Key terms

  1. Informational influence — conforming to others because we believe they have accurate information we lack to attain a group goal.
    1. ex. following the directives of an effective leader because their expertise is perceived as valid.

Names and studies

  1. Eagly & Chravala (1986) — demonstrated that gender-congruent knowledge drives influence; men conformed on fashion while women conformed on sports.
