3.6 How to Feel Better: Coping With Negative Emotions

Thought suppression

Attempting to ignore negative affect is an ineffective approach because suppressed thoughts eventually return to conscious awareness.

Key terms

  1. Thought suppression — attempting to avoid or deny negative affect by pushing it out of conscious awareness.
    1. putting an exam out of one’s mind instead of preparing for it

Names and studies

  1. Wegner et al. (1987) — instructed participants to avoid thinking of a white bear, finding the thought repeatedly popped into mind.
  2. Gross & Levenson (1997) — found suppression fails long-term because recalling the event to suppress it causes emotions to return when energy is low.
  3. Baumeister (1991) — speculated that maladaptive behaviors like drug abuse or binge eating are used to escape self-awareness.

Emotional expression

Communicating feelings through talking or writing is a healthy alternative to bottling them up and provides physical health benefits.

Key terms

  1. Self-expression — talking or writing about emotions and reactions to negative events to ourselves or others.
    1. physical pain reduction — a positive outcome of self-expression.

Names and studies

  1. Pennebaker & Beall (1986) — assigned students to write about trauma, finding they had fewer health center visits over a 6-month period.
  2. Petrie et al. (2004) — showed that daily writing about emotional states increased immune system functioning.
  3. Uysal & Lu (2011) — found that self-expression was associated with experiencing less physical pain.

Delayed gratification

Self-regulation involves using cognitive capacities to override immediate impulses to reach long-term goals.

Key terms

  1. Self-regulation — the process of setting goals and using cognitive and affective capacities to reach them.
  2. Delayed gratification — overriding the impulse for immediate rewards to obtain greater rewards later.
  3. Serotonin — a neurotransmitter where low levels are linked to impulsiveness and a preference for small immediate rewards.

Names and studies

  1. Mischel et al. (1989) — found children who waited for two marshmallows had better SAT scores and social skills later in life.

Resource depletion

The effort required to regulate emotional responses consumes finite resources, leading to reduced performance on subsequent tasks.

Key terms

  1. Emotion regulation — the effortful task of increasing or decreasing one's emotional responses.
  2. Regulatory depletion — a state where the effort to control emotions consumes capacity, leaving less for other tasks.

Names and studies

  1. Muraven, Tice, & Baumeister (1998) — used a muscle metaphor to show that participants regulating emotions during a movie had less hand-grip strength.
  2. Vohs & Heatherton (2000) — found that resisting tempting snacks led to poorer performance on subsequent energy-demanding tasks.

Cultural differences in social support

Cultural values influence how individuals seek assistance, with Westerners using it for goals and Easterners avoiding it for harmony.

Key terms

  1. Social support — the perception or reality that one is cared for and has assistance available from others.
    1. Direct effects — occur when others provide specific advice, financial help, or help in determining how to deal with a stressor.
    2. Emotional effects — occur from knowing that others are available if they are needed.

Names and studies

  1. Taylor et al. (2004) — found American students use social support (57.1%) more than Koreans (39.3%) who worry about disrupting group harmony.
  2. Koopman et al. (1998) — found women with higher social support experienced less depression when adjusting to a cancer diagnosis.
  3. Ashton et al. (2005) — demonstrated that social support provides a buffering effect for physical health symptoms in AIDS patients.

Hedonic adaptation

Individuals habituate to major life changes and return to a moderate level of well-being through resilience.

Key terms

  1. Hedonic adaptation — the process of returning to a moderate level of happiness after a positive or negative event.
  2. Habituation — the process of becoming accustomed to positive outcomes so their emotional effect wears off over time.
  3. Resilience — the ability to bring coping skills into play when negative events occur to restore happiness levels.

Names and studies

  1. Brickman, Coates, & Janoff-Bulman (1978) — showed that $50,000 lottery winners were not significantly happier than non-winners over time.
  2. Small et al. (2001) — found that people enjoy a second chocolate bar less than the first due to adaptation.
  3. Twenge et al. (2003) — reviewed well-being studies and found that couples without children report higher happiness than those with children.

Perceived control

The belief that one can influence environmental stressors leads to constructive responses and significantly lower levels of stress.

Key terms

  1. Perceived control — the belief that one can influence or stop potential stressors in their environment.
  2. Self-efficacy — the belief in one's ability to carry out actions that produce desired outcomes.

Names and studies

  1. Glass, Reim, & Singer (1971) — found that participants who believed they could stop a loud noise experienced less stress even if they never did.
  2. Rodin (1986) — demonstrated that nursing home patients who could choose their own daily activities experienced less stress.

Optimism and health

Maintaining an optimistic outlook and a hardy attitude contributes to physical recovery and increased longevity.

Key terms

  1. Optimism — a general tendency to expect positive outcomes in the future.
  2. Hardiness — an individual-difference measure involving a positive attitude toward stressors and a tendency to take direct action.

Names and studies

  1. Peterson et al. (1998) — found that college optimism levels predicted fewer accidental or violent deaths over the subsequent 50 years.
  2. Levy & Myers (2005) — found that older adults with positive perceptions of aging lived an average of 8 years longer than those with negative views.
  3. Antoni et al. (2001) — showed that training cancer patients in optimism decreased their fatigue and enhanced their positive outlook.

Affective forecasting

Human predictions regarding future emotional states are consistently inaccurate as people overestimate the intensity and duration of reactions.

Key terms

  1. Affective forecasting — the process of predicting one's future emotional states and their duration.
  2. Focalism — a bias where people focus only on one specific event and ignore regular activities that influence happiness.
  3. Durability bias — the tendency to overestimate how long emotional reactions to future events will last.

Names and studies

  1. Wilson & Gilbert (2005) — found that people generally overestimate their emotional reactions to future events.
  2. Wilson et al. (2000) — demonstrated that focusing on regular daily activities makes predictions about the influence of future events less extreme.
  3. Argyle (1999) — estimated that external factors like wealth and health account for only 15% to 20% of total well-being scores.

Resource restoration

Self-regulation capacity can be increased through practice and replenished through the experience of positive affect.

Key terms

  1. Self-regulation training — the practice of performing difficult tasks to improve overall self-control capacity.
    1. maintaining good posture or avoiding swearing

Names and studies

  1. Baumeister et al. (2006) — showed that students who practiced difficult tasks like exercising later performed better on laboratory self-regulation tests.
  2. Tice et al. (2007) — found that people who watched a funny video clip were better at subsequent self-regulation tasks.

Health behaviors and emotion regulation

Maintaining health requires using cognitive resources and emotion regulation to avoid dangerous but temporarily pleasurable behaviors.

Key terms

  1. Binge drinking — the act of having five or more drinks in one sitting, leading to severe negative health consequences.
  2. Physical inactivity — a lack of regular exercise that, combined with poor diet, represents a major threat to health.

Names and studies

  1. Fryar et al. (2009) — reported that more than 60% of children try smoking before age 18, with over half failing to quit.
  2. Hillman et al. (2008) — showed that regular physical exercise has positive influences on cognitive processes and academic performance.
  3. Miller et al. (2007) — found that binge-drinking students are more likely to engage in other risky behaviors like drug use or suicide attempts.
