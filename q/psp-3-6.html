3.6 How to Feel Better: Coping With Negative Emotions

  1. Thought suppression
    • Thought suppression is a common but counterproductive strategy involving the volitional attempt to avoid, ignore, or deny the presence of negative affect.
    • Key terms:
    • Thought suppression: The deliberate effort to push intrusive negative thoughts or distressing feelings out of conscious awareness.

e.g., Attempting to put a high-stakes exam out of mind instead of engaging in active preparation.

  • Names and studies: Wegner et al. (1987) demonstrated "paradoxical effects" in the 5-minute "white bear" experiment, where suppression efforts actually increased the frequency of the thought.
  1. Emotional expression
    • Externalizing negative thoughts and feelings through verbal or written disclosure is associated with significant longitudinal improvements in mental and physical health.
    • Key terms:
    • Emotional expression: The therapeutic process of "opening up" about emotional reactions to stressors rather than cognitively bottling them.

Type: Maintaining a daily journal to document and process one’s internal emotional states.

  • Names and studies: Pennebaker and Beall (1986) found trauma-writing students had fewer health center visits over 6 months, while Petrie et al. (2004) linked such writing to improved immune function.
  1. Delay of gratification
    • This self-regulatory capacity involves the cognitive ability to override immediate impulses in order to secure more valuable rewards in the future.
    • Key terms:
    • Self-regulation: The process of setting goals and utilizing cognitive and affective capacities to guide behavior toward those objectives.

Component: Establishing specific, measurable goals to direct behavior and monitor progress.

  • Names and studies: Mischel et al. (1989) showed that preschoolers who delayed eating a snack for a larger reward later exhibited higher SAT scores and superior social adeptness as adolescents.
  1. Ego depletion
    • The ego depletion model posits that self-control functions as a finite internal resource that becomes progressively exhausted through continued exertion.
    • Key terms:
    • Self-control: The effortful management of impulses or the regulation of emotional responses to maintain goal-directed behavior.

Metaphor: Self-control acts like a muscle that exhibits fatigue and reduced strength following heavy or prolonged use.

  • Names and studies: Muraven et al. (1998) found regulating movie reactions reduced hand-grip stamina (change of -25.1 for increase, -18.49 for decrease, vs. -1.57 for the control group).
  1. Neurotransmitter influence
    • Impulsive behaviors and preferences for immediate rewards are rooted in biological patterns influenced by specific neurochemical concentrations.
    • Key terms:
    • Serotonin: A neurotransmitter central to the regulation of emotional responses and the capacity for behavioral self-control.

Associated behavior: Low serotonin levels are tied to impulsiveness, violence, and a preference for small immediate rewards over larger delayed rewards.

  • Names and studies: Bizot et al. (1999) found that low serotonin levels in animals predicted a significant preference for immediate gratification over delayed reward.
  1. Psychological hardiness
    • Hardiness is an individual-difference measure characterized by a positive outlook and the tendency to take direct, constructive action toward stressors.
    • Key terms:
    • Hardiness: A personality trait involving a positive response to stress and a commitment to learning from negative life events.

Hardy behavior: Engaging in proactive coping strategies and maintaining rigorous self-care routines during periods of high stress.

  • Names and studies: Maddi, Kahn, and Maddi (1998) demonstrated that hardiness training focusing on effective coping increased life satisfaction and significantly decreased self-reported stress levels.
  1. Dispositional optimism
    • Dispositional optimism is the stable tendency to maintain a general expectation that future outcomes will be positive and favorable.
    • Key terms:
    • Optimism: A general expectation that good things will happen in the future, facilitating better coping during stressful periods.

Health outcome: Optimistic individuals have been shown to make significantly faster recoveries following major surgeries.

  1. Self-efficacy: The belief in one's ability to execute actions, such as regular exercise, required to produce desired results.

Behavioral result: High self-efficacy is a robust predictor of success in quitting smoking and losing weight.

  • Names and studies: Peterson et al. (1998) found that college students who were optimistic between 1936 and 1940 experienced lower 50-year mortality rates and fewer accidental deaths.
  1. Cultural social support
    • Cultural "selfways" significantly influence how individuals perceive the social costs and benefits of seeking assistance from others during stress.
    • Key terms:
    • Collectivist cultures: Eastern societies where individuals are viewed as fundamentally connected, making help-seeking a potential risk to group harmony.

Concern: A fear that seeking support might disrupt social equilibrium, elicit criticism, or cause a loss of face.

  1. Individualist cultures: Western societies that view the self as independent and use relationships to promote personal goals.

Use for relationships: Utilizing social networks as an appropriate means to provide direct help or financial assistance for individual aims.

  • Names and studies: Taylor et al. (2004) found American students were more likely to use social support than Korean students, who feared social disapproval or burdening the group.
  1. Hedonic adaptation
    • Hedonic adaptation is the psychological process where people habituate to new circumstances until their well-being returns to a baseline moderate level.
    • Key terms:
    • Resilience: The inherent ability of individuals to recover from negative events by bringing coping skills into play.

Function: Utilizing personal resources to restore a sense of happiness and stability after significant life changes.

  • Names and studies: Brickman, Coates, and Janoff-Bulman (1978) found lottery winners weren't happier than controls, and accident victims were not as unhappy as might be expected.
  1. Affective forecasting
    • Affective forecasting refers to the systematic difficulty people have when trying to accurately predict the intensity and duration of their future emotional states.
    • Key terms:
    • Focalism: A cognitive bias where people focus solely on a single event, leading to a "durability bias" in emotional predictions.

Example: Forgetting that one will still be occupied with regular activities like working or socializing regardless of a specific life event.

  • Names and studies: Wilson and Gilbert (2005) research indicates that people consistently overestimate how much positive or negative events will influence their long-term well-being.
  1. Material wealth
    • Once basic physiological needs are met, increases in personal wealth have a diminishing impact on long-term happiness due to habituation and comparison.
    • Key terms:
    • Social comparison: The process of evaluating one’s status relative to peers, which shifts as one's own wealth increases.

Example: Wealthy individuals comparing their success to other affluent people rather than to the general population.

  • Names and studies: Layard (2005) showed in Figure 3.8 that while income rose in the U.S., France, and Japan from 1946–1990, the percentage of "very happy" people was stagnant.
  1. Self-control training
    • Self-regulation is a skill that can be strengthened through regular practice of disciplined tasks, leading to a "transfer" effect on unrelated behaviors.
    • Key terms:
    • Physical training: A metaphor suggesting that the regulatory "muscle" improves with consistent use on difficult tasks.

Exercise: Practicing disciplined behaviors such as maintaining good posture or consciously avoiding swearing.

  • Names and studies: Baumeister et al. (2006) found that practicing posture or avoiding swearing improved laboratory test performance on tasks like maintaining a diet or completing a puzzle.
  1. Emotion regulation
    • Emotion regulation involves the cognitive effort required to manage emotional responses to avoid maladaptive behaviors used to "escape the self."
    • Key terms:
    • Maladaptive behaviors: Dysfunctional actions, such as binge drinking or drug abuse, used as mechanisms to "escape the self" (Baumeister, 1991).

Example: Utilizing substance abuse to distract from the discrepancy between the ideal self and the actual self.

  • Names and studies: Vohs and Heatherton (2000) demonstrated that resisting the temptation of snacks (chocolates and cookies) caused participants to give up sooner on subsequent, unrelated tasks.
  1. Buffering effect
    • Social support serves as a protective layer that diminishes the physiological and psychological impact of high-stress situations on health.
    • Key terms:
    • Direct effects: Tangible assistance, such as financial aid or information sharing, provided by a social network to help manage a stressor.

Example: Receiving money or specific advice from a friend during a financial or academic crisis.

  1. Emotional effects: The psychological benefit derived from the mere perception that others are available to provide support if needed.

Example: Feeling an increased sense of self-worth simply because of the presence of a supportive social circle.

  • Names and studies: Ashton et al. (2005) identified social support as a significant longitudinal predictor of reduced physical symptoms in persons living with HIV/AIDS.
