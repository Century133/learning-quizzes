3.6 How to Feel Better: Coping With Negative Emotions

Thought suppression

Attempting to ignore or deny negative affect frequently leads to a paradoxical increase in those very thoughts.

Key terms

  • Thought suppression: The act of attempting to push negative feelings or thoughts out of consciousness to avoid dealing with them.
  • Distracting behaviors: Activities such as going for a run or watching TV that people use to avoid facing troubles directly.

Names and studies

  • Wegner et al. — Instructed participants to avoid thinking of a white bear, then found the thought kept popping into mind despite suppression attempts.

Emotional expression

Confronting troubles by letting negative thoughts and feelings out is a healthy alternative to bottling up emotions.

Key terms

  • Opening up: The process of talking or writing about emotions or reactions to negative events to provide mental and physical health benefits.

Names and studies

  • Pennebaker and Beall — Assigned students to write about trauma or trivial topics, then those writing about trauma visited the health center less for illnesses during the following 6 months.
  • Petrie et al. — Tracked the immune function of patients writing about emotional states, then found that daily writing increased immune system functioning.
  • Uysal and Lu — Investigated the link between self-expression and pain, then found that self-expression was associated with experiencing less physical pain.

Delayed gratification

Self-regulation involves the capacity to override immediate impulses to seek larger rewards at a later time.

Key terms

  • Self-regulation: The process of setting goals and using our cognitive and affective capacities to reach those goals.

Names and studies

  • Mischel et al. — Offered children one snack immediately or two if they could wait, then found those who waited had better SAT scores and social skills later in life.

Resource depletion

Exerting self-control functions like a muscle that becomes tired and less effective when it is used too much.

Key terms

  • Emotion regulation: The process of either increasing or decreasing our emotional responses.

Names and studies

  • Muraven, Tice, and Baumeister — Tasked participants with regulating emotions during a movie, then found reduced hand-grip strength, showing self-control is a finite resource.

Cultural differences in social support

Western and Eastern cultures utilize social networks differently due to varying perceptions of independence and group harmony.

Key terms

  • Social support: The perception of having positive social relationships with others who are available to help deal with stressors.

Names and studies

  • Taylor et al. — Compared coping strategies, then found Americans were significantly more likely to use social support while Koreans avoided it to preserve group harmony.

Hedonic adaptation

Individuals tend to habituate to new circumstances, leading their happiness levels to return to a stable baseline over time.

Names and studies

  • Brickman, Coates, and Janoff-Bulman — Interviewed lottery winners and paralyzed accident victims, then found winners were not happier than controls and victims were less unhappy than expected.

Perceived control

The belief that one can influence or stop potential stressors significantly reduces the physical and mental impact of stress.

Key terms

  • Self-efficacy: The belief in our ability to carry out actions that produce desired outcomes.
  • Hardiness: An individual-difference measure characterized by being positive about stressful events and taking direct action to learn from them.

Names and studies

  • Glass, Reim, and Singer — Told participants they could stop a loud noise during a study, then found those who believed they had control experienced less stress.

Optimism and health

General expectations of positive outcomes are linked to improved recovery from illness and increased longevity.

Key terms

  • Optimism: A general tendency to expect positive outcomes in the future.

Names and studies

  • Peterson et al. — Followed college students for 50 years, then found those with positive outlooks were less likely to die, particularly from accidental or violent deaths.
  • Levy and Myers — Studied attitudes of older adults, then found those with positive views of aging lived an average of almost eight years longer.

Affective forecasting

Affective forecasting reveals a significant "durability bias" where individuals overestimate the long-term impact of life events on their happiness.

Names and studies

  • Wilson et al. — Asked participants to focus on regular future activities before making predictions, then found their predictions about the influence of events were less extreme.

Resource restoration

Negative effects of self-regulatory depletion can be offset by positive affect and restorative activities.

Names and studies

  • Tice et al. — Provided participants with funny video clips after a self-regulation task, then found that positive affect restored self-regulatory strength for subsequent tasks.
