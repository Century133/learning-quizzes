3.6 How to Feel Better: Coping With Negative Emotions

Thought suppression

Thought suppression is the attempt to ignore, avoid, or deny negative affect and internal difficulties.

Key terms

  1. Thought suppression — attempting to avoid or ignore negative emotions and thoughts.
  2. Distraction — engaging in behaviors like running or watching TV to avoid self-awareness.
    1. ex. drug abuse or binge eating — extreme maladaptive escape mechanisms.

Names and studies

  1. Wegner et al. (1987) — asked participants to not think of a white bear, showing that the thought repeatedly returned despite instructions.
  2. Gross & Levenson (1997) — found that suppressing emotions fails in the long run because it requires facing the event to make the attempt.

Emotional expression

Releasing negative affect through verbal or written expression facilitates substantial mental and physical health gains.

Key terms

  1. Emotional expression — the process of talking or writing about reactions to negative events.
  2. Self-concealment — the opposite of expression, often associated with increased physical pain.

Names and studies

  1. Pennebaker & Beall (1986) — assigned students to write about trauma, resulting in fewer health center visits over the following six months.
  2. Petrie et al. (2004) — showed that daily writing about emotional states increases immune system functioning.
  3. Uysal & Lu (2011) — found that self-expression is associated with experiencing less physical pain.

Delay of gratification

Self-regulation involves the ability to resist immediate impulses to obtain greater rewards in the future.

Key terms

  1. Self-regulation — setting goals and using cognitive and affective capacities to reach them.
  2. Delay of gratification — overriding an immediate impulse to obtain a greater reward later.
  3. Serotonin — a neurotransmitter linked to the ability to prefer large later rewards over small immediate ones.

Names and studies

  1. Mischel et al. (1989) — tested children with marshmallows, finding that those who waited had better SAT scores and social skills later in life.
  2. Bizot et al. (1999) — linked low levels of serotonin in animals to a preference for small immediate rewards.

Regulatory depletion

Self-regulation consumes limited cognitive energy, leaving fewer resources for subsequent tasks.

Key terms

  1. Emotion regulation — the work involved in either increasing or decreasing emotional responses.
  2. Regulatory depletion — the state where self-control resources are exhausted after use.

Names and studies

  1. Muraven, Tice, & Baumeister (1998) — showed participants who regulated emotions (incl. increasing/decreasing) had less physical strength afterward.
  2. Vohs & Heatherton (2000) — found resisting temptations (ex. chocolate) led to poorer performance on subsequent tasks vs. control.
  3. Tice et al. (2007) — demonstrated that watching a funny video can help restore self-regulation after depletion.

Perceived control

The belief in one’s ability to influence or stop a stressor significantly reduces the level of stress experienced.

Key terms

  1. Self-efficacy — the belief in our ability to carry out actions that produce desired outcomes.
  2. Perceived control — the perception that one can influence or stop a stressor.

Names and studies

  1. Rodin (1986) — found that nursing home patients who chose their daily activities experienced less stress.
  2. Glass, Reim, & Singer (1971) — showed that the belief one could stop a loud noise reduced stress even if the option was never used.

Cultural social support

Cultural orientation influences whether individuals seek social support or avoid it to protect group harmony.

Key terms

  1. Individualist cultures — cultures where relationships are used to promote individual goals.
  2. Collectivist cultures — cultures where individual goals are seen as a means to promote relationships.

Names and studies

  1. Taylor et al. (2004) — found American students use social support more than Korean students, who fear disrupting group harmony.
  2. Kim, Sherman, & Taylor (2008) — noted that in Eastern cultures, asking for help can be seen as making inappropriate demands.

Material wealth influence

After meeting basic needs for food and shelter, additional material wealth has a negligible impact on long-term happiness.

Key terms

  1. Minimum wealth level — the baseline income needed for food and shelter, after which happiness gains diminish.

Names and studies

  1. Layard (2005)/Figure 3.8 — shows that while personal income in the U.S. has risen dramatically since 1946, the percentage of "very happy" people has remained flat.
  2. Csikszentmihalyi (1999) — found that regardless of current earnings, people consistently believe more money would make them "really" happy.

Affective forecasting

Humans are generally inaccurate when predicting the intensity and duration of their future emotional reactions to events.

Key terms

  1. Affective forecasting — the process of predicting one's future emotional state.
  2. Habituation — adapting to positive outcomes until well-being returns to baseline (ex. enjoyment of second chocolate bar).
  3. Focalism — focusing only on one event and forgetting other things that stay the same when predicting happiness.

Names and studies

  1. Wilson & Gilbert (2005) — found that people generally overestimate their emotional reactions to future events.
  2. Brickman, Coates, & Janoff-Bulman (1978) — interviewed lottery winners and accident victims, finding their happiness levels eventually returned to baseline.
  3. Wilson et al. (2000) — found that focusing on regular future activities made emotional predictions less extreme.

Optimism and health

General expectations of positive outcomes are strongly correlated with superior physical health and recovery.

Key terms

  1. Optimism — a general tendency to expect positive outcomes in the future.

Names and studies

  1. Peterson et al. (1998) — showed college optimism levels predicted health and lower accidental death rates 50 years later.
  2. Carver et al. (2005) — found that optimists make faster recoveries from major surgeries/illnesses w/ better well-being outcomes.
  3. Antoni et al. (2001) — trained cancer patients in optimism, resulting in less fatigue and better outlooks.

Hardiness

Hardiness is an individual-difference trait that protects against stress through direct action and learning from negative events.

Key terms

  1. Hardiness — a trait characterized by taking direct action to understand stressors and learning from negative events.

Names and studies

  1. Kobasa, Maddi, & Kahn (1982) — defined hardiness as a protective factor linked to both optimism and self-efficacy.
  2. Maddi, Kahn, & Maddi (1998) — demonstrated that "hardiness training" effectively increases life satisfaction and decreases reported stress.
