3.6 How to Feel Better: Coping With Negative Emotions

This topic covers the psychological strategies, research-backed mechanisms, and social factors that influence our ability to manage negative affect and improve long-term well-being.

Key Terms

Self-regulation — The process of setting goals and using cognitive and affective capacities to reach those goals, including the control of one's emotions.

Optimism — A general tendency to expect positive outcomes in the future.

Self-efficacy — The belief in one's own ability to carry out specific actions that lead to desired outcomes.

Hardiness — A personality trait characterized by a positive outlook on stressors, taking direct action to understand causes, and attempting to learn from negative events.

Social Support — The perception or experience of having positive social relationships with others who can provide emotional or direct assistance.

Names and Studies

Wegner et al. (White bear suppression) — Participants were instructed to avoid thinking of a white bear for five minutes; the study showed that the thought repeatedly returned to consciousness, demonstrating that suppression is often ineffective and leads to a rebound effect.

Pennebaker and Beall (Writing about trauma) — Students who wrote about traumatic events initially experienced negative moods but later visited the student health center significantly less over the following six months compared to those who wrote about trivial topics.

Walter Mischel (The marshmallow/snack test) — Children were offered one snack immediately or two if they could wait; those able to delay gratification grew up to have higher SAT scores and better social adeptness.

Muraven, Tice, and Baumeister (Hand-grip and movie emotion regulation) — Participants who were required to either increase or decrease their emotional responses while watching a sad movie showed a significant decrease in physical hand-grip strength afterward, suggesting self-control is a limited, "muscle-like" resource.

Glass, Reim, and Singer (Controllable vs. uncontrollable noise) — Participants who believed they had a button to stop a loud noise experienced less stress than those who did not, even if they never actually used the button, proving the psychological power of perceived control.

Peterson et al. (College optimism and 50-year health outcomes) — This longitudinal study found that college students who utilized a "catastrophizing" pessimistic style were significantly more likely to have died from accidental or violent causes fifty years later.

Taylor et al. (Cultural differences in support-seeking between Americans and Koreans) — Research showed that Korean students were less likely to seek social support than Americans because they perceived seeking help as a threat to group harmony or a risk of losing face.

Brickman, Coates, and Janoff-Bulman (Lottery winners and accident victims) — This study found that lottery winners were no happier than they had been in the past once the initial excitement wore off, demonstrating hedonic adaptation.

Wilson et al. (Focalism/focusing on specific events in future predictions) — Participants showed a "durability bias" by overestimating the impact of future events; however, they made more accurate predictions when prompted to consider daily routines that would remain unchanged.

Application Quiz (Finding to Implication)

1. In Wegner’s study, participants were told not to think about a white bear while they performed a task. What was the observed result?

A. Participants successfully cleared their minds of the image for the full duration.

B. The image of the bear repeatedly popped into their minds despite their efforts.

C. Participants became so focused on the bear they forgot to perform the secondary task.

D. The suppression of the bear led to a permanent decrease in the bear's perceived "yummy" quality.

2. Pennebaker’s research required students to write about their most traumatic life experiences. What was the long-term health implication for these students?

A. They reported more frequent illness due to the stress of reliving the trauma.

B. They showed no change in health outcomes compared to those who wrote about trivial topics.

C. They visited the student health center less frequently over the next six months.

D. They developed a permanent aversion to writing and self-expression.

3. In Mischel's "marshmallow test," some children were able to wait for a second snack while others ate the first one immediately. What did this childhood behavior predict about their adulthood?

A. Those who ate the snack immediately were more likely to become successful chefs.

B. Those who waited for the second snack had higher SAT scores and were more socially adept.

C. There was no correlation between childhood snack choices and adult behavior.

D. Those who waited were more likely to experience chronic anxiety in social situations.

4. In Muraven’s study, participants squeezed a hand-grip exerciser before and after regulating their emotions during a movie. What did the results suggest about the nature of self-control?

A. Regulating emotions is an effortless cognitive process that saves physical energy.

B. Suppressing emotions causes a spike in adrenaline that increases physical strength temporarily.

C. Self-control acts like a muscle that becomes fatigued after use, leading to reduced stamina.

D. Physical strength is the primary predictor of a person's ability to regulate their emotions.

5. Tice et al. conducted a study where participants watched a funny video after completing a difficult task. What was the behavioral outcome?

A. The participants became too distracted by the humor to complete any further work.

B. The positive mood helped restore regulatory resources, improving performance on subsequent tasks.

C. Watching a funny video provided no more restorative benefit for self-regulation than watching a neutral video.

D. The humor caused a total loss of impulse control, making participants give up on all subsequent goals.

6. In the Glass et al. study on loud noise, one group was told they could press a button to stop the noise but were asked not to use it unless necessary. What was the psychological impact?

A. They experienced higher stress because they were constantly thinking about the button.

B. They experienced less stress than the group without a button, simply because they perceived they had control.

C. They became angry at the researchers for giving them a button they weren't supposed to use.

D. They performed worse on tasks because they were waiting for the noise to stop.

7. Peterson’s longitudinal study tracked college students' optimism levels for 50 years. What was the specific finding regarding the health of those who "catastrophized" negative events?

A. They lived longer because they were more prepared for the worst-case scenarios.

B. They were significantly more likely to die from accidental or violent causes.

C. They had higher rates of heart disease but lower rates of accidental injury.

D. Their pessimistic outlook had no effect on their physical health or lifespan.

8. Levy and Myers researched how positive self-perceptions of aging affect longevity in older adults. What was the result?

A. People with positive attitudes toward aging lived an average of 8 years longer.

B. Positive attitudes improved mental health and life satisfaction but had no impact on physical longevity.

C. Older adults who were positive about aging were more likely to ignore medical symptoms.

D. Attitudes toward aging had a minor effect that was negated by the participants' marital status.

9. Taylor’s cultural study examined why Korean students seek social support less often than American students. What was the primary reason found?

A. Korean students do not believe that talking to others is effective for stress.

B. Korean students are naturally more independent and do not need social support.

C. Seeking support is viewed as a risk to group harmony and may cause a "loss of face."

D. American students have more friends available to provide support than Korean students.

10. Research has compared national wealth (GDP) and happiness levels in Western countries over several decades. What has this data shown?

A. As GDP has increased, national happiness levels have increased at the same rate.

B. Increases in national wealth have led to a significant decrease in overall happiness.

C. Despite dramatic increases in buying power and income, overall happiness has remained flat.

D. Happiness only increases in nations that have a declining GDP.

11. Brickman’s study compared the happiness of lottery winners to a control group. What was the finding after the initial excitement wore off?

A. Winners remained significantly happier than the control group for the rest of their lives.

B. Winners became much unhappier than the control group due to the stress of managing money.

C. Winners were not significantly happier than they had been in the past.

D. Winners were only happy if they gave all their money away to charity.

12. Wilson’s "focalism" study asked people to predict their future feelings while considering their daily routines. How did this affect their predictions?

A. Their predictions became more extreme as they realized how boring their lives were.

B. Their predictions became less extreme and more accurate regarding the impact of future events.

C. They were unable to make any predictions once they started thinking about their daily chores.

D. They predicted they would be happy regardless of any future negative events.

13. According to the review by Twenge regarding marital satisfaction and children, what is the reported finding?

A. Couples with children report significantly higher levels of happiness than those without.

B. Couples without children actually report being happier than those who have children.

C. Marital happiness is tied more to a couple's income level than to the presence of children.

D. Having children has no measurable impact on a couple’s reported well-being.
