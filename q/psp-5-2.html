Speech rate

Speed of delivery impacts perceived authority and reduces the listener's ability to develop cognitive counterarguments.

Key terms

  1. Expertise — the perception that a communicator is knowledgeable based on confident, fast, and straightforward delivery.
  2. Counterarguments — cognitive rebuttals that are harder to generate when the listener must process information at high speed.

Names and studies

  1. MacLachlan & Siegel (1980) — sped up TV commercials by deleting very small segments of speech, then showed that faster delivery made communications more persuasive.
  2. Moore, Hausknecht, & Thamodaran (1986) — manipulated the speed of audio messages, then showed that faster speech enhanced the perceived expertise of the source.

Processing motivation

Effective persuasion depends on whether the recipient is motivated and able to invest cognitive effort in the message.

Key terms

  1. Spontaneous processing — a quick, direct response where persuasion occurs based on obvious or enjoyable cues rather than the message itself.
    1. Peripheral/Heuristic processing — alternative terms for the quick, non-deliberative route of attitude change.
  2. Thoughtful (Central/Systematic) processing — a controlled route involving careful cognitive elaboration of the message's meaning and validity.

Names and studies

  1. Shelley Chaiken (1980) — compared students with high versus low involvement in a topic, then showed that low-involvement students were persuaded by likeability rather than argument quality.

Affective states

Mood and positive emotional associations determine the depth of cognitive processing and the level of message acceptance.

Key terms

  1. Associational learning — the process of linking a product with positive features, such as humor or beauty, within an advertisement.
  2. Positive affect — a mood state signaling "things are OK," leading to less careful information processing and higher acceptance of spontaneous appeals.

Names and studies

  1. Sinclair, Mark, & Clore (1994) — examined how mood affects information processing, then showed that the spontaneous approach is most effective when people are happy.

Message framing

The presentation of information as either a potential gain or a potential loss significantly influences its effectiveness.

Key terms

  1. Negative framing — presenting information to suggest that failing to adopt a specific behavior will result in harm to the self.

Names and studies

  1. Banks, Salovey, Greener, and Rothman (1995) — compared different mammogram recruitment messages, then found that emphasizing the risk of not getting a screening was more effective than emphasizing the benefits.
  2. Ito et al. (1998) — measured brain responses to different affective stimuli, then showed that the brain responds more strongly to negative affect than to positive affect.

Fear appeals

High-anxiety messaging can drive behavioral change, provided the recipient feels capable of addressing the highlighted threat.

Key terms

  1. Self-efficacy — the belief that one has the ability to take the necessary actions to rectify the danger highlighted by a fearful message.

Names and studies

  1. Witte & Allen (2000) — conducted a meta-analysis on the use of fear in public health, then showed that fearful messages are generally persuasive for driving behavior change.
  2. Passyn & Sujan (2006) — studied the interaction of fear and self-accountability, then showed that fear appeals work best when they provide specific instructions on how to respond.

Relevance cues

The personal importance of a topic determines whether a recipient utilizes the thoughtful or spontaneous route of persuasion.

Key terms

  1. High personal relevance — a state where a topic affects the listener directly, motivating them to engage in thoughtful processing of message content.
  2. Low personal relevance — a state where a topic is unimportant, causing the listener to rely on spontaneous cues like source expertise.

Names and studies

  1. Petty, Cacioppo, and Goldman (1981) — manipulated relevance regarding a new exam policy, then showed that highly involved students were influenced by argument strength rather than source expertise.

Source memory

Attitudes can change over time as the mental link between a message and its original source begins to decay.

Key terms

  1. Sleeper effect — attitude change occurring when we forget to discount a message from an untrustworthy source as the memory of the source fades.

Names and studies

  1. Kumkale & Albarracín (2004) — reviewed findings on temporal persuasion, then showed that people eventually remember message content more clearly than source credibility.
  2. National Enquirer anecdote — a researcher recalled a movie star story from an unreliable tabloid, then showed how content is accepted once the source discounting is forgotten.

Resistance techniques

Methods used to strengthen existing attitudes can make individuals more resilient against future attempts at persuasion.

Key terms

  1. Inoculation — building defenses by exposing a person to a weak attack on their attitude to help them develop counterarguments for future appeals.

Names and studies

  1. McGuire (1961) — tested supportive versus refutational defenses, then showed that mild attacks on beliefs helped people resist subsequent, stronger persuasion attempts.
  2. Compton & Pfau (2005) — reviewed the application of resistance theory, then showed that practicing counterarguments makes an attitude stronger and more defined.

Choice freedom

Individuals often respond with rebellion when they feel their autonomy or ability to make decisions is being threatened.

Key terms

  1. Psychological reactance — a strong emotional rebellion triggered when a person feels their freedom of choice is being taken away.

Names and studies

  1. Erceg-Hurn and Steed (2011) — investigated the impact of graphic cigarette warnings on smokers, then showed that high-pressure messaging can trigger reactance and reduce warning impact.

Subliminal priming

Messaging presented below the threshold of conscious awareness has negligible and highly conditional effects on actual behavior.

Key terms

  1. Subliminal advertising — flashing brand images or messages so quickly that the recipient is unaware they have received a message.

Names and studies

  1. Karremans, Stroebe, and Claus (2006) — primed students with "Lipton Ice" messages, then showed the prime only increased brand choice for participants who were currently thirsty.
  2. Trappey’s (1996) — conducted a meta-analysis of 23 research studies, then showed that subliminal advertising has a negligible effect on actual consumer choice.

Communicator bias

Perceived source self-interest and message sidedness impact how much weight a listener gives to the information presented.

Key terms

  1. Discounting — the tendency to ignore a message when the communicator is perceived to have an external, self-serving motive.
  2. Message Sidedness — the extent to which a communicator acknowledges or ignores potential problems and counterarguments.
    1. One-sided versus two-sided messages — the contrast between presenting only one perspective or acknowledging both, where the former can appear unfairly biased.

Names and studies

  1. Eagly, Wood, & Chaiken (1978) — analyzed causal inferences about sources, then showed that communicators arguing in their own self-interest are seen as less trustworthy.
  2. Wood & Eagly (1981) — studied messages that go against external causes, then showed that arguing against self-interest makes the communicator's internal belief seem even more powerful.

Social liking

Interpersonal attraction and perceived commonality between the source and recipient can bypass the critical evaluation of a message.

Key terms

  1. Likable Sources — communicators that people tend to trust, often leading to message acceptance without critical evaluation.
    1. Attractive communicators — sources who use physical appeal to create positive associations and increase the likelihood of message acceptance.
    2. Similar communicators — sources who share values with the audience, such as teenagers used in ads for teens or elderly people for older adults.

Names and studies

  1. Tupperware parties example — examined social selling within friend groups, then showed that liking the salesperson was more influential than the product's actual nature.
  2. Priester & Petty (2003) — researched the impact of celebrity spokespeople, then showed that perceived trustworthiness allows messages to be accepted without critical evaluation.

Strategic forewarning

Advance notice of a persuasive attempt allows individuals to bolster existing beliefs, though it may trigger pre-emptive changes for unimportant topics.

Key terms

  1. Forewarning — informing people they will soon receive a persuasive message, giving them time to practice resistance and develop counter-responses.

Names and studies

  1. Wood & Quinn (2003) — synthesized research on warnings, then showed forewarning defends attitudes we care about but may cause pre-emptive change for topics we do not.
