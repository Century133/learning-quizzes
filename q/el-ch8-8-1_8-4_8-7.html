Sense and Denotation

Lexical meaning is interpreted either through mental representations held by the speaker or through the specific entities that a word references in the physical world.

Key terms

  1. Sense — The mental representation of a word's meaning or the lexical semantics stored within a speaker's mind.
  2. Denotation — The relationship where a linguistic expression points to a specific object or entity in the actual world.

At-issue meaning

At-issue meaning constitutes the primary, literal content of an utterance that serves as the immediate topic of discussion.

Key terms

  1. At-issue meaning — The main, literal layer of communication that identifies the current topic being addressed.

Non-at-issue meaning

Beyond the literal surface, sentences carry additional layers of meaning, such as presuppositions and implicatures, which are classified as non-at-issue content.

Key terms

  1. Non-at-issue meaning — Communicative content that exists outside the literal surface of the sentence, including implied or assumed information.

Truth-conditional meaning

While often used interchangeably with at-issue meaning in external literature, the term "truth-conditional meaning" is technically complex because non-at-issue elements like presuppositions also relate to a sentence's truth requirements.

Key terms

  1. Truth-conditional meaning — A technical term used in broader linguistics to designate at-issue content, though it is often avoided in this text to prevent confusion with presuppositions.

Presupposition

Presuppositions function as the necessary background assumptions that must be true for a sentence to be appropriately produced and understood.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition — Information that a speaker treats as already established or true prior to the production of an utterance.

1.1 The definite determiner "the" triggers the assumption that the referenced object exists.

Implicature

An implicature is a meaning suggested by an utterance within a specific context that does not follow from formal logical entailment.

Key terms

  1. Implicature — A non-entailed meaning suggested by an utterance based on the specific circumstances and context of the discourse.

Speaker commitment

In discourse, speakers signal their level of sincerity and the stance they take regarding their utterances, both of which are potentially subject to later retraction.

Key terms

  1. Commitment — The stance or sincerity a speaker exhibits during discourse regarding the propositions they have expressed.

At-issue vs. non-at-issue

Linguists apply a diagnostic test using sentential negation to isolate the literal at-issue meaning, which is negated, from non-at-issue content, which survives the negation.

Key terms

  1. Sentential negation — The application of the phrase "it is not the case that..." to a sentence to identify its at-issue content.

1.1 At-issueness diagnostic — A test where the at-issue meaning is canceled by negation while the non-at-issue meaning remains true.

Contextual Influence

Interpretation is inherently context-dependent, as the same sentence can generate entirely different implicatures depending on the environment and the shared knowledge of the participants.

Key terms

  1. Context-dependency — The linguistic property wherein an implicature's interpretation varies according to the specific situation or the participants' background information.

Cancellable Meanings

Cancellability is the defining trait of implicatures where the suggested meaning can be denied or retracted without resulting in a logical contradiction of the sentence's literal entailments.

Key terms

  1. Cancellability — The diagnostic property where a suggested meaning can be false while the literal sentence remains logically true.

1.1 Cancellable meaning — A non-entailed implicature that can be false even if the main proposition of the sentence is true.

Conversational Implicature

Conversational implicatures refer specifically to implied meanings that arise from the systematic application of cooperative social and communicative rules.

Key terms

  1. Conversational implicature — A subset of implicatures generated specifically through the application of standard conversational principles.

Logical Entailment

Entailment is a formal logical relationship where the truth of one proposition necessitates the truth of another, a state that remains constant regardless of context.

Key terms

  1. Entailment — A meaning that is necessarily true and cannot be cancelled or altered by contextual factors.

Flouting maxims

Flouting occurs when a speaker intentionally and obviously breaks a conversational rule to signal that the listener should look for a non-literal, hidden meaning.

Key terms

  1. Flouting — Appearing to break a maxim in a very obvious way to prompt the listener to derive an implicature.

1.1 Sarcasm — A common communicative outcome resulting from the deliberate flouting of the Maxim of Quality.

Maxim violations

A violation occurs when a conversational rule is broken in a way that suggests the speaker is being uncooperative, typically leading to a breakdown in discourse.

Key terms

  1. Violation — Breaking a conversational rule in a manner that disrupts discourse or makes the speaker appear uncooperative.

Communicative strategies

Cultural and individual variations determine one's native discourse strategies, which dictate how effectively a person recognizes and processes communication via implicature.

Key terms

  1. Native discourse strategies — Personal or cultural communicative frameworks that involve varying degrees of ability to process and understand non-literal implicatures.

Quality Maxim

The Maxim of Quality serves as the bedrock of cooperative communication, requiring participants to make contributions they believe to be true and supported by sufficient evidence.

Key terms

  1. Maxim of Quality — The conversational requirement that participants provide truthful information backed by factual evidence.

Violating vs. flouting

While a violation typically necessitates discourse repair to reconcile facts, flouting is a productive strategy where a speaker intentionally breaks a rule to generate meaning through overt non-compliance.

Key terms

  1. Discourse repair — The linguistic process of clarifying facts or straightening out communication after a maxim violation occurs.
  2. Deliberate violation — The overt act of flouting a maxim to generate a specific implicature rather than to cause a communicative failure.
