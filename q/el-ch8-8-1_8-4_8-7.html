  1. Sense and Denotation

Sense and denotation are distinct mechanisms for interpreting lexical meaning through mental concepts or external reference.

Key terms

  • Sense: Conceptual lexical semantics residing within the minds of speakers.
    • 'Strawberry' as a mental concept or visual image in the speaker's head.
  • Denotation: The relationship where a word refers to a specific entity in the actual world.
    • 'Strawberry' and 'Ode'imin' sharing a physical fruit referent despite distinct senses.
  • At-issue meaning

At-issue meaning constitutes the literal, surface-level content that serves as the primary topic of discourse.

Key terms

  • At-issue meaning: The core proposition or main claim of an utterance.
  • Sentential negation test: Primary diagnostic using "it is not the case that..." to cancel at-issue content.
    • Negating "Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries" cancels the purchase event.
  • Non-at-issue meaning

Non-at-issue meaning encompasses propositional content existing outside the surface claim that survives sentential negation.

Key terms

  • Non-at-issue meaning: Information not targeted by the primary diagnostic of negation.
    • Categories: Presuppositions (must survive negation) and implicatures (may survive or disappear).
  • Truth-conditional meaning

Truth-conditional meaning maps the significance of a sentence to the factual conditions required for at-issue content to be true.

Key terms

  • Truth-conditional meaning: At-issue content directly evaluated for factual truth or falsity.
  • Non-truth-conditional meaning: Non-at-issue content, such as implicatures, excluded from literal truth-conditions.
    • Note: Presuppositions are truth-related as requirements for sentential felicity.
  • Presupposition

Presuppositions are background assumptions that must be accepted as fact for an utterance to be felicitous.

Key terms

  • Presupposition: Information assumed true at the time of production; necessarily survives negation.
  • Trigger: Definite determiners like "the" signal existence and uniqueness of a referent.
    • "The department head forgot" presupposes the existence of a specific head and an approval document.
  • Speaker commitment

Speaker commitment refers to the speaker's sincerity and propositional responsibility for a claim within the discourse.

Key terms

  • Speaker commitment: The obligation of a participant to stand by the truth of their utterance.
  • Retraction: The act of withdrawing a prior commitment (e.g., "I didn't mean it").
  • Implicature

Implicatures are suggested meanings inferred from context that are not logically entailed by the utterance.

Key terms

  • Implicature: A non-entailment suggested by an utterance based on environmental and social context.
    • Saying "Mounissa bought 10lbs of strawberries" implies she intends to make jam.
  • Contextual Influence

Contextual influence determines how environmental circumstances and participant knowledge shift implied meanings.

Key terms

  • Context-dependency: The property where implicatures fluctuate while logical entailments remain static.
    • Telling a history enthusiast "This is a history book" implies they will enjoy it.
  • Cancellable Meanings

Cancellable meanings can be explicitly retracted or denied without generating a logical contradiction.

Key terms

  • Cancellability: The ability to deny a suggestion while maintaining the truth of the literal statement.
    • "This is a history book, but it is not long" cancels the length suggestion.
  • Conversational Implicature

Conversational implicatures are suggestions arising from the logic and cooperative expectations of social interaction.

Key terms

  • Conversational Implicature: Implied meaning derived from the universal logic of social cooperation.
  • Origin: Underlying rules participants use to navigate and interpret discourse.

Names and studies

  • H. P. Grice (1975): Proposed the seminal framework for how implicatures arise from conversational logic.
  • Logical Entailment

Logical entailments are meanings that must be true if the statement is true and cannot be cancelled.

Key terms

  • Entailment: Context-independent facts logically necessitated by the proposition; non-cancellable.
  • Contradiction: Retracting an entailment creates a logical clash (symbolized as bot).
    • "This is a book about China" logically necessitates the fact "This is a book."
  • Maxim violations

Maxim violations involve the quiet or non-obvious breaking of conversational rules, causing discourse breakdown.

Key terms

  • Maxim violation: Uncooperative, quiet rule-breaking, such as violating Quality via contradiction.
  • Discourse repair: The process of straightening out facts after a conversation becomes anomalous.
    • Bo contradicting his location (Ottawa vs. Toronto) necessitates a repair of facts.
  • Flouting maxims

Flouting maxims is the intentional and obvious breaking of rules to signal that an implicature is being made.

Key terms

  • Flouting: Openly appearing to violate a maxim to force the listener to infer a non-literal meaning.
    • Sarcasm: A primary method of flouting where the literal statement is obviously false.
  • Quality Maxim

The Quality Maxim requires that participants provide truthful information supported by adequate evidence.

Key terms

  • Maxim of Quality: The rule that discourse participants should not say what they believe to be false.
    • Saying "Toronto, Saskatchewan" flouts Quality to signal sarcasm regarding obvious facts.
  • Communicative strategies

Communicative strategies are paralinguistic tools used to ensure non-at-issue meanings are correctly signaled.

Key terms

  • Strategies: Linguistic and physical cues including Intonation, Facial expressions, and Direct clarification.
    • Exaggerated tone or a wink indicates the speaker is flouting a rule rather than lying.
