Ch8 Exam · 8.1, 8.4, 8.7

At-issue meaning

This is the literal, core content of an utterance that is the main topic of discussion.

Key terms

  1. At-issue meaning — the literal or surface meaning representing the main topic or issue being discussed.

1.1 Sentential negation test — identification of at-issue content using the "it is not the case that..." frame to see what meaning is cancelled.

Sense and Denotation

This is the distinction between mental representations and real-world references.

Key terms

  1. Sense — the mental lexical semantics or mental image of a word, such as a visual image or a botanical definition.
  2. Denotation — the specific object or entity the word points to in the actual world.

2.1 Reference example — "ode’imin" and "strawberry" share a denotation (the fruit) but possess different mental senses.

Non-at-issue meaning

This is meaning that exists outside the surface-level literal claim and survives negation.

Key terms

  1. Non-at-issue meaning — propositional content that is not the main "issue" and cannot be targeted or cancelled by sentential negation.

1.1 Primary types — categorization of non-at-issue content into implicatures and presuppositions.

Truth-conditional meaning

This is an alternative linguistic term used to describe at-issue content.

Key terms

  1. Truth-conditional meaning — propositional content vulnerable to truth-value challenges, which a "no, that is false" response can target.

Presupposition

This is the background information that must be accepted as true for a sentence to be coherent.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition — information assumed to be true already when a sentence is uttered, remaining true even if the sentence is negated.

1.1 Survival diagnostic — the assumption in "The department head forgot" that a head exists persists even under negation.

Speaker commitment

This is the level of sincerity or responsibility a speaker takes for their utterance in discourse.

Key terms

  1. Speaker commitment — a commitment made during discourse regarding the speaker's sincerity or responsibility for their utterance.
  2. Retraction — the act of taking back a commitment, typically signaled by saying "I didn't mean it."

Implicature

This is a suggested meaning that is not logically entailed but arises from context.

Key terms

  1. Implicature — a non-entailment suggested by a sentence based on surrounding context and conversational goals.

1.1 Contextual suggestion — stating "Mounissa bought 10lbs of strawberries" to imply she is making jam based on known preferences.

Meaning types

This is the broad categorization of linguistic meaning versus discourse-act meaning.

Key terms

  1. Linguistic meaning — meaning derived directly from the sense or denotation of the lexical items used.
  2. Discourse meaning — meaning concerning what a speaker is doing in a conversation, such as making or retracting a commitment.

At-issue diagnostic

This is the mechanical test used to separate literal claims from suggestions.

Key terms

  1. Sentential negation — the "it is not the case that..." test used to cancel at-issue meaning while non-at-issue meaning survives.

1.1 Diagnostic application — negating "Mounissa will buy strawberries" cancels the purchase act while the presupposition "there are strawberries" remains.

Contextual Influence

This is the way environment and participant knowledge shape non-literal meaning.

Key terms

  1. Context-dependency — the phenomenon where identical sentences carry different implicatures based on the speaker's knowledge of the listener.

1.1 Interpretive shift — "This is a history book" implying a recommendation or a warning based on whether the listener likes history.

Cancellable Meanings

This is the property of meaning that allows a speaker to take back a suggestion without logical conflict.

Key terms

  1. Cancellability — the ability to negate a previous suggestion or implicature without creating a logical contradiction.

1.1 Scalar implicature — the statement "She has three kids—in fact four," where the "only three" suggestion is successfully retracted.

1.2 Clarification example — stating "The report is on your desk" and adding "but I haven't finished the conclusion" to negate the "finished" implicature.

Conversational Implicature

This is suggestions that arise specifically from the rules of human interaction.

Key terms

  1. Conversational implicature — suggested meanings that arise specifically out of underlying conversational rules and human interaction.

Logical Entailment

This is information that is necessarily true and cannot be cancelled or influenced by context.

Key terms

  1. Logical entailment — a relationship where if the sentence is true, the piece of information must also be true and cannot be retracted.

1.1 Necessity example — the fact that "This is a book about China" necessarily entails "This is a book."

Maxim violations

This is the quiet or uncooperative breaking of conversational rules.

Key terms

  1. Maxim violation — an uncooperative, often quiet move where a rule is broken in a way that makes the discourse anomalous.

1.1 Sign of violation — the participant intuition that "something has gone wrong" or the speaker is being uncooperative.

Flouting maxims

This is the deliberate and obvious breaking of rules to communicate a specific subtext.

Key terms

  1. Flouting — appearing to violate a rule in an obvious way to create an implicature that the speaker intends to be noticed.
  2. Sarcasm — a common form of flouting often indicated by exaggerated intonation or facial expressions to ensure the violation is recognized.

Quality Maxim

This is the conversational requirement to provide truthful and evidenced information.

Key terms

  1. Maxim of Quality — the rule requiring speakers to be truthful and avoid saying things they know to be false or lack evidence for.

1.1 Illustrative violation — Bo providing contradictory locations (Ottawa versus Toronto) in a single discourse.

Communicative strategies

This is the individual variations in how people use and interpret non-literal language.

Key terms

  1. Native discourse strategy — a person's inherent style of using pragmatics, including variations in how they perceive or use sarcasm.
  2. Directness — the strategy of stating an implicature out loud to avoid confusion or to explicitly clarify intended sarcasm.

Repair

This is the discourse process used to correct factual errors or rule violations.

Key terms

  1. Discourse repair — the straightening out of facts or clarification required before a conversation can continue after a violation.

1.1 Signal — the use of prompts like "Wait, what?" to indicate that the discourse has broken down and requires correction.

Cooperative Principle

This is the foundational set of rules that allow participants to understand each other.

Key terms

  1. Cooperative Principle — the Gricean framework of underlying rules (Maxims) that participants follow to work out and interpret meanings successfully.
