At-issue Meaning

At-issue meaning constitutes the literal, surface-level proposition of a sentence that forms the primary topic of discussion.

  1. At-issue meaning — The core propositional content or "surface" meaning of an utterance that is the direct object of the discourse.

1.1. ex. "Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries" — This directly claims a specific purchase event will occur.

  1. Negation Test — A diagnostic tool that uses sentential negation (e.g., "it is not the case that...") to isolate at-issue content.

2.1. ex. Saying "It is not the case that Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries" cancels the literal claim, demonstrating that the purchase itself is the at-issue content.

Sense and Denotation

Meaning is bifurcated into mental representations held by the speaker and the actual entities those representations pick out in reality.

  1. Sense — The mental representation or lexical semantics of an expression stored in a speaker's mind.

1.1. ex. Two coworkers discussing a "strawberry" may focus on different senses, such as its botanical definition versus its visual image.

  1. Denotation — The specific entity or object in the real world to which a linguistic expression refers.

1.2. ex. The Ojibwe term "ode’imin" and the English word "strawberry" share a denotation because they point to the same physical fruit.

Non-at-issue Meaning and Truth Conditions

Non-at-issue meaning encompasses layers of communication, such as presuppositions and implicatures, that function outside the primary literal claim.

  1. Non-at-issue meaning — Any meaning conveyed by an utterance that is not part of its "surface" content and is resistant to sentential negation.

1.1. ex. "Mounissa likes strawberries" — A meaning suggested by context that is not literally stated in the purchase claim.

  1. Truth-conditional meaning — A technical term for at-issue meaning, as it defines the conditions under which a sentence is true.

2.1. Pedagogical Note: While some linguists use "non-truth-conditional" for non-at-issue content, this textbook avoids it because presuppositions do affect truth (they must be true for the sentence to be uttered).

  1. Truth-conditional content — The specific portion of a statement targeted by direct denials such as "No, that is false."

Presupposition

A presupposition is background information that is taken for granted and must be accepted as fact for an utterance to be felicitous.

  1. Presupposition — Information assumed to be true prior to an utterance, often triggered by specific lexical items.

1.1. Lexical Trigger: The definite determiner "the" in "the discounted strawberries" triggers the presupposition that such strawberries exist.

  1. The "Survives Negation" Property — Unlike at-issue meaning, a presupposition must remain true even if the main sentence is negated.

2.1. ex. "It is not the case that the department head forgot" still asserts that a department head exists; the presupposition mandatory survives the negation.

Implicature and Contextual Influence

Implicatures are non-entailed suggestions that arise through the interplay of literal meaning and conversational context.

  1. Implicature — A meaning suggested by an utterance that is not logically entailed and can shift based on environmental factors.

1.1. ex. Stating Mounissa bought 10lbs of strawberries implies she is making jam if the listener knows she likes jam, but it implies she is selling at a farmer’s market in a different context.

  1. Conversational Implicature — A suggested meaning that arises specifically from the application of Gricean conversational rules.

2.1. ex. A friend concluding they will like a book because you mention its topic matches their interests.

  1. Context-dependency — The phenomenon where the same sentence carries different meanings based on shared knowledge between the speaker and listener.

3.1. ex. "This is a book about China" implies "You will like it" to a history enthusiast, but implies "You will not like it" to someone who finds world history tedious.

Cancellability and Entailment

Linguistic meaning is distinguished by whether a specific inference can be retracted without creating a logical contradiction.

  1. Cancellability — The property of non-at-issue meanings (specifically implicatures) that allows them to be retracted or clarified without making the sentence nonsensical.

1.1. ex. "She has three kids—in fact four" — The implicature "only three" is cancelled by the second clause without contradiction.

  1. Cancellable meanings — Information that is not necessarily true and can be explicitly denied by the speaker to avoid misunderstanding.

2.1. ex. "The report is on your desk, but I still need to write the conclusion" — Clarifies that "on your desk" does not mean "finished."

  1. Logical Entailment — A necessary truth that follows automatically from a sentence and cannot be cancelled.

3.1. ex. "This is a book about China" necessarily entails "This is a book"; denying this (e.g., "...but it is not a book") creates a logical contradiction (⊥).

The Cooperative Principle and Maxims

Successful discourse relies on the "Gricean" assumption that participants work together to be understood.

  1. Cooperative Principle — The overarching expectation that participants follow specific rules (Gricean Maxims) to ensure a successful exchange.
  2. Maxim of Quality — The rule requiring speakers to be truthful and avoid making claims for which they lack adequate evidence.

2.1. Violation of Quality: The act of lying or quietly contradicting established facts, which leads to a breakdown in discourse.

  1. Scalar Implicature — A specific implicature where using a weaker term on a scale (e.g., "some") suggests that a stronger term (e.g., "all") is false.

Interactional Strategies: Flouting vs. Violating

The way speakers break conversational rules determines whether they are being uncooperative or simply nuanced.

  1. Flouting Maxims — A "loud" or obvious violation of a maxim intended to prompt the listener to look for a hidden, non-literal meaning.

1.1. ex. Sarcastic use of "Toronto, Saskatchewan" — Since both parties know no such city exists, the speaker is flouting Quality to signal irony.

  1. Violating Maxims — A "quiet" or non-obvious breaking of a rule, often leading to confusion or a total breakdown of the conversation.

2.1. ex. Bo telling Aya he lives in Ottawa and then later saying Toronto without acknowledgement; this is uncooperative and requires repair.

  1. Speaker Commitment — The level of sincerity or responsibility a speaker assumes for their claims.

3.1. ex. Retracting a commitment by saying, "I said coffee is as tasty as tea, but I didn't mean it."

Discourse Management and Repair

When conversational rules are broken or misunderstood, speakers use specific strategies to restore clarity.

  1. Non-verbal cues — Indicators such as exaggerated intonation, winks, or facial expressions that signal a speaker is flouting a maxim rather than lying.
  2. Discourse Repair — The process of straightening out facts or resolving contradictions to fix an anomalous conversation.

2.1. ex. Signalling a breakdown by asking "Wait, what?" after a speaker contradicts themselves.

  1. Directness Strategy — A clarifying method where a speaker utters the implicature out loud to ensure the meaning is not missed by those who do not use sarcasm.

3.1. ex. Following a joke with "I was being sarcastic" to resolve potential ambiguity.
