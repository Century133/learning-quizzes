At-issue meaning

At-issue meaning constitutes the primary literal claim of an utterance and represents the main topic of discussion in a discourse.

Key terms

  1. At-issue meaning - the surface-level literal content that serves as the main topic of discussion in a sentence.
    • Negation test: a diagnostic where sentential negation ("it is not the case that...") cancels only the at-issue meaning.

Sense and Denotation

Lexical meaning is divided between the mental concepts associated with words and the actual entities they refer to in the world.

Key terms

  1. Sense - the internal mental representation or lexical semantics of a word stored within a speaker's head.
  2. Denotation - the relationship where a word points to a specific, real-world entity in the actual world.
    • Ode’imin and strawberry: an example where Ojibwe and English terms share a denotation but have different mental senses.

Non-at-issue meaning

Non-at-issue meaning involves communicative content that is suggested or assumed rather than being part of the primary surface claim.

Key terms

  1. Non-at-issue meaning - content that is not the main topic of discussion and remains true even if the sentence is negated.

Truth-conditional meaning

Truth-conditional meaning refers to the specific set of conditions that must be met for the literal claim of a statement to be true.

Key terms

  1. Truth-conditional meaning - an alternative linguistic term for at-issue meaning because it is the content targeted by a denial of truth.

Presupposition

A presupposition is information that both participants must accept as background fact for an utterance to be logically coherent.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition - information assumed to be true beforehand that projects through or survives the negation of a sentence.
    • Definite determiner "the": a trigger that presupposes the existence of a specific entity, such as a department head.

Implicature

An implicature is a meaning suggested by a speaker based on the context of an utterance rather than being logically required.

Key terms

  1. Implicature - a non-entailment suggested by a sentence that can change or disappear depending on the surrounding environment.
    • Strawberry jam: the suggestion that someone is making jam based on the context of purchasing 10lbs of fruit.

Speaker commitment

Speaker commitment refers to the sincerity of the speaker and their obligation to stand by the claims made during discourse.

Key terms

  1. Speaker commitment - the expression of a speaker's sincerity or their obligation to be held responsible for a claim made in discourse.
    • "New developer is very fast": an example where a speaker might use "I didn't mean it" to retract a commitment to a suggested meaning.

Non-at-issue and Implicature

Implicatures are categorized as a specific subset of meaning that exists outside of the at-issue, truth-conditional content.

Key terms

  1. Implicatures - a specific type of non-at-issue meaning that is suggested by context rather than stated literally.

Contextual Influence

The interpretation of an implicature is dictated by the environment and what the speaker knows about the listener's background.

Key terms

  1. Context-dependence - the phenomenon where the same sentence carries different meanings depending on what the speaker knows about the hearer.
    • History of China book: an utterance that implies a recommendation to a history enthusiast but a warning to someone who dislikes history.

Cancellable Meanings

Cancellability is the property that allows a speaker to take back or clarify a suggested meaning without creating a logical contradiction.

Key terms

  1. Cancellability - the property of implicatures that allows them to be denied or retracted, unlike non-cancellable entailments.
    • "The report is on your desk": a statement where the implied "it is finished" can be cancelled by adding "but I still need to write the conclusion."

Conversational Implicature

Conversational implicatures are meanings derived by listeners based on the assumption that the speaker is following cooperative rules.

Key terms

  1. Conversational implicature - suggested meanings that arise specifically out of the underlying rules people use to interact and understand each other.

Names and studies

  1. H.P. Grice (1975) - established the theoretical framework for how conversational rules and the cooperative principle generate suggested meanings.

Logical Entailment

An entailment is a meaning that is a necessary consequence of a sentence and cannot be denied if the original sentence is true.

Key terms

  1. Entailment - a necessary consequence of a sentence that cannot be cancelled without creating a logical contradiction.
    • "This is a book": a necessary logical entailment of the sentence "This is a book about the history of China."

Scalar implicature

Scalar implicatures occur when a speaker chooses a weaker term on a scale to suggest that a stronger term does not apply.

Key terms

  1. Scalar implicature - the suggestion that a more intense or higher-quantity term on a scale of values is not true.
    • "some" vs "not all": using the word "some" to imply the stronger claim "all" is false, which is cancellable without contradiction.

Maxim violations

A maxim violation occurs when a speaker quietly and uncooperatively breaks a conversational rule, leading to a breakdown in discourse.

Key terms

  1. Violation - a quiet or uncooperative breaking of a maxim that causes the conversation to feel anomalous or require clarification.
  2. Repair - the process of straightening out facts and correcting the discourse after a rule violation has been detected.

Flouting maxims

Flouting is the blatant and obvious breaking of a conversational rule used to prompt the listener to find a hidden, non-literal meaning.

Key terms

  1. Flouting - appearing to violate a rule so obviously that the listener assumes the speaker is still being cooperative and looks for an implicature.
    • Sarcasm: an example of flouting the truth to imply the opposite of what is literally said.

Communicative strategies

Communicative strategies encompass the varied internal rules and external signals individuals use to manage and interpret interaction.

Key terms

  1. Discourse strategies - the varied internal rules for interaction that can differ between native speakers and guide how meaning is conveyed.
    • Non-verbal cues: signaling mechanisms like intonation, winks, and facial expressions that indicate a speaker is flouting a maxim.

Quality Maxim

The Maxim of Quality is a conversational rule requiring speakers to be truthful and provide only information supported by evidence.

Key terms

  1. Maxim of Quality - the requirement for speakers to say only what they believe to be true and have adequate evidence for.
    • "No, Toronto, Saskatchewan": a dialogue snippet used to blatantly flout the quality maxim to signal sarcasm to a listener.

Names and studies

  1. H.P. Grice (1975) - defined the Maxim of Quality as a fundamental component of the cooperative principle in human communication.
