At-issue meaning

At-issue meaning refers to the literal, surface-level content of a sentence that serves as the main topic of discussion.

Key terms

  1. At-issue meaning — the main, literal claim of a sentence that is the target of sentential negation.
    1. ex. "Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries" — the literal event of the purchase.
  2. Sentential negation — a diagnostic tool using "it is not the case that..." to identify at-issue content.
    1. ex. "It is not the case that Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries" — cancels the purchase claim.

Sense and Denotation

Sense and denotation distinguish between the mental representation of a word and its specific reference in the real world.

Key terms

  1. Sense — the lexical meaning or mental concept associated with a word in a speaker's head.
    1. ex. A coworker thinking of a botanical definition or a mental image of a fruit.
  2. Denotation — the specific entity or object that a word points to in the actual world.
    1. ex. "Ode’imin" and "strawberry" — both point to the same physical fruit.

Non-at-issue meaning

Non-at-issue meaning is supplemental information that is not part of the surface claim and survives negation.

Key terms

  1. Non-at-issue meaning — supplemental meaning that is not the main "issue" and survives sentential negation.
  2. Diagnostic — unlike implicatures which may disappear, presuppositions always survive sentential negation.

Names and studies

  1. Potts (2015) — explored the relationship and formal properties of presupposition and implicature.

Truth-conditional meaning

This is a term used outside the textbook for at-issue meaning to identify content that determines truth or falsity.

Key terms

  1. Truth-conditional meaning — an external term for at-issue meaning used to denote content tied to truth values.
    1. Function — the specific part of a statement a listener rejects when saying "That is not the case."

Presupposition

Presuppositions are background assumptions that must be accepted as true for a sentence to be used appropriately.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition — information assumed to be true already that survives the negation diagnostic.
    1. ex. "The department head forgot" — presupposes that a department head exists.
  2. Survival property — background assumptions remain true even if the surface-level claim is negated.
    1. ex. "It is not the case that Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries" — still assumes strawberries exist.

Implicature

Implicatures are suggested meanings that arise from the context of a conversation rather than from logical entailment.

Key terms

  1. Implicature — a non-entailment suggested by a sentence based on the specific conversational context.
  2. Scalar implicature — a suggestion where using a term like "some" implies "not all."
    1. ex. "Mounissa bought 10lbs of strawberries" — implies she is making a large amount of jam.

Speaker commitment

This refers to the level of sincerity or the discourse obligations a speaker undertakes when producing an utterance.

Key terms

  1. Speaker commitment — a speaker's reference to the sincerity or obligation made during a discourse.
  2. Retraction — the act of using "I didn't mean it" to withdraw a commitment to one's sincerity.

Linguistic Meaning

Linguistic meaning concerns literal sense and denotation, contrasting with cultural or situational interpretations.

Key terms

  1. Linguistic meaning — the literal sense and denotation of words as defined within a language system.
    1. ex. "Ode’imin and strawberry mean the same thing" — concerns shared lexical semantics.
  2. Non-linguistic meaning — cultural signs or functional points that do not rely on lexical semantics.
    1. ex. A tea stalk floating vertically — refers to cultural significance rather than word sense.

Cancellable Meanings

Cancellability is the property of certain meanings that allows them to be retracted without creating a logical contradiction.

Key terms

  1. Cancellability — the property of taking back a suggested meaning by adding a specific clarification.
    1. ex. "The report is on your desk, but I still need to write the conclusion" — cancels the "finished" suggestion.

Contextual Influence

The meaning of an utterance changes based on the environment and the knowledge shared by the participants.

Key terms

  1. Context-dependency — the phenomenon where the same sentence implies different things based on shared knowledge.
    1. ex. Telling a history enthusiast a book is about China — implies they will like it.
  2. Opposite implicature — a context shift that changes a suggestion to the exact opposite of its previous instance.
    1. ex. Telling a history hater a book is about China — implies they will not like it.

Conversational Implicature

Conversational implicatures emerge from the underlying rules and expectations that govern human interaction.

Key terms

  1. Conversational implicature — suggested meaning arising from the underlying rules of human interaction.

Names and studies

  1. H.P. Grice (1975) — the primary figure who defined the rules of logic and conversation.

Logical Entailment

Logical entailment refers to meanings that are necessarily true and cannot be retracted without contradiction.

Key terms

  1. Logical entailment — a meaning that must be true if the sentence is true and is never context-dependent.
  2. Entailment test — adding "but it is not the case that..." to an entailment results in a clash (perp).

Maxim violations

Violations occur when conversational rules are broken unnoticeably, leading to uncooperative discourse.

Key terms

  1. Maxim violation — breaking a rule in a quiet or unnoticeable way that makes the speaker uncooperative.
  2. Discourse repair — the process of fixing a conversation after a violation, often signaled by "Wait, what?".

Flouting maxims

Flouting is the deliberate and obvious violation of a conversational rule used to communicate an indirect message.

Key terms

  1. Flouting — appearing to violate a maxim in an obvious, deliberate way to create an implicature.
    1. ex. Saying "No, Toronto, Saskatchewan" — flouting truth to imply the location is obviously Ontario.

Communicative strategies

These tools allow speakers to signal flouting or to clarify intended meanings to ensure understanding.

Key terms

  1. Non-verbal cues — indicators like exaggerated intonation or winks used to show a maxim is being flouted.
  2. Direct clarification — stating an implied meaning out loud to ensure sarcasm is not taken literally.

Quality Maxim

The Maxim of Quality is the conversational requirement that speakers be sincere and provide truthful information.

Key terms

  1. Maxim of Quality — the conversational rule requiring speakers to be sincere and provide truthful information.
    1. ex. Bo claiming to live in Ottawa and then Toronto — a violation of the rule of truthfulness.
