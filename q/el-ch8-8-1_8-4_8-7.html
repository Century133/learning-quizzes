This technical study sheet provides a categorical framework for analyzing how meaning is constructed, communicated, and interpreted in human discourse. It focuses on the distinction between the literal surface of language and the supplemental layers of meaning generated through interaction and context.

At-issue meaning

At-issue meaning refers to the main, literal claim made by a sentence which serves as the primary topic of discussion.

Key terms

  1. At-issue meaning: The surface-level meaning of a sentence representing the core topic of discussion. In a pedagogical context, "issue" refers to the "topic," not a negative "problem."
    • Example: In the claim "Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries," the at-issue meaning is the literal event of Mounissa purchasing that fruit.
  2. At-issueness diagnostic: A linguistic test where sentential negation ("it is not the case that...") is applied to a sentence; if the specific meaning is canceled by this negation, it is identified as at-issue. (Note: This diagnostic is a test for identification, not the definition of the term itself.)

Sense and Denotation

Linguistic meaning can be divided into internal mental representations held by speakers and the external entities those words refer to in the physical world.

Key terms

  1. Sense: The lexical semantics or mental image an individual associates with a word.
    • Example: One person’s sense of "strawberry" may be a botanical definition, while another’s may be a specific visual image of the fruit.
  2. Denotation: The relationship where a word points to a specific object or entity in the actual world.
    • Example: The Ojibwe word ode’imin and the English word strawberry have different senses but share the same denotation because they point to the same fruit.

Non-at-issue meaning

Non-at-issue meaning consists of supplemental information or suggestions that exist outside the literal surface of an utterance.

Key terms

  1. Non-at-issue meaning: Meaning that is not the primary focus of an utterance and is not targeted or canceled by sentential negation.
    • Primary types:
      1. Implicatures
      2. Presuppositions

Truth-conditional meaning

Truth-conditional meaning defines the aspects of a statement that determine its verifiable truth or falsity within a discourse.

Key terms

  1. Truth-conditional meaning: The specific aspect of meaning that a "no, that is false" response can target and deny.
  2. Non-truth-conditional meaning: A synonym used in external linguistic literature to describe non-at-issue meaning.

Presupposition

A presupposition is the background information that must be accepted as fact by discourse participants for an utterance to be sensible.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition: Information that a reader or listener must accept as a fact before the sentence is uttered.
    • Example: In the sentence "The department head forgot to sign the approval," the definite determiner the triggers the presupposition that a specific department head exists AND that an approval document exists.

Implicature

An implicature is a suggestion or inference derived from an utterance that is not a formal entailment and is not necessarily true.

Key terms

  1. Implicature: A non-entailed meaning suggested by a sentence based on the specific context of the utterance.
    • Example: Saying "Mounissa bought 10lbs of strawberries" implies she is making jam, though this is a suggestion and not a guaranteed fact.

Speaker commitment

Speaker commitment involves the responsibility and sincerity obligations a speaker takes on regarding the claims they make during a conversation.

Key terms

  1. Speaker commitment: The discourse obligation a speaker has for the sincerity of their statement.
    • Example: A speaker may attempt to retract a commitment by stating, "I didn't mean it," which targets their sincerity rather than the sense of the words.

Presupposition vs Implicature

These two categories of non-at-issue meaning are technically distinguished by their relationship to sentential negation.

Key terms

  1. Survival test: A diagnostic showing that sentential negation cancels at-issue meaning, while presuppositions must survive (remain true). Implicatures are not targeted by negation but may disappear or change depending on the resulting context.

Denotation

In the analysis of quantity and logic, denotation serves as a tool to determine how language maps to sets of objects.

Key terms

  1. Denotation: The pointing function used to analyze the mechanics of quantificational meaning in sentences like "Every strawberry is red."

Cancellable Meanings

Cancellability is the property of being able to take back a suggestion without creating a logical contradiction.

Key terms

  1. Cancellability: A defining property of implicatures where the speaker can deny the suggested meaning without a logical clash.
    • Example: "This is a book about China, but it is not long" is logically sound because the suggestion that history books are long is cancellable.

Contextual Influence

The meaning of a single sentence can shift dramatically depending on the environment and the participants' shared knowledge.

Key terms

  1. Context-dependency: The phenomenon where the same sentence implies different things based on the speaker's knowledge of the hearer.
    • Example: Telling Josh "This is a book about China" implies he will like it if he is a history enthusiast, but implies he will dislike it if he hates history.

Conversational Implicature

Conversational implicatures arise from the underlying, shared rules that humans use to interact and understand one another.

Key terms

  1. Conversational implicature: Suggested meanings that emerge from the cooperative principles and rules of human discourse.

Logical Entailment

An entailment is a relationship of necessity between sentences where the truth of one logically guarantees the truth of another.

Key terms

  1. Entailment: A relationship where one sentence being true necessitates the truth of another; unlike implicatures, entailments are not context-dependent and cannot be retracted without contradiction.
    • Example: "This is a book about the history of China" necessitates the truth of "This is a book."

Cancellability and Flouting

Speakers utilize specific linguistic strategies to clarify when a meaning was intended only as a suggestion.

Key terms

  1. Clarification strategies: The addition of phrases such as "but I didn't mean it that way" or "but I still need to write the conclusion" to indicate that an implicature was a suggestion, not a fact.

Scalar Implicature

Scalar implicature is a specialized suggestion based on a perceived scale of quantity or intensity.

Key terms

  1. Scalar implicature: A suggestion where the use of a term on a quantity scale or scale of strength (like "some") implies the higher terms on that scale (like "all") are false.

Flouting maxims

Flouting is a deliberate, obvious, and "loud" departure from conversational rules intended to communicate an indirect meaning.

Key terms

  1. Flouting: Appearing to violate a maxim in a very noticeable way so the listener realizes an implicature is being created.
    • Example: Sarcasm involves flouting the Maxim of Quality. The speaker uses non-verbal cues (like intonation or facial expressions) to ensure the listener notices the "lie" and looks for the implied meaning.

Maxim violations

A violation occurs when a speaker quietly fails to follow a conversational rule, leading to a breakdown in discourse.

Key terms

  1. Violating: A quiet, non-obvious failure to follow a maxim. This is uncooperative and often perceived as a lie or error until the contradiction is realized.
    • Example: Bo quietly violating the Maxim of Quality by providing contradictory locations (saying he lives in Ottawa, then later Toronto) without signaling a flout.
  2. Repair: The process of straightening out facts when a violation is revealed, typically signaled by the listener saying, "Wait, what?".

Communicative strategies

Speakers use a variety of signals to distinguish between being literal, lying, or being indirect.

Key terms

  1. Non-verbal cues: The use of intonation, facial expressions, or winks to signal that a speaker is flouting a rule rather than violating it. These cues prevent the hearer from thinking the speaker is simply being uncooperative or lying.

Quality Maxim

The Maxim of Quality concerns the expectation of truthfulness and factual accuracy in contributions to a conversation.

Key terms

  1. Maxim of Quality: The rule that speakers should be truthful and not say things they believe to be false.
    • Example: Saying "Toronto, Saskatchewan" is a breach of this maxim because both parties know no such city exists in that province.

Names and studies

  • Grice (1975) — Established that implicatures are cancellable without contradiction, distinguishing them from entailments. He used the example "She has three kids—in fact four" to illustrate that while "three" implies "exactly three," it can be retracted to "four" without the logical clash an entailment would cause.
