At-issue meaning

At-issue meaning: literal, surface-level claim or main topic of discussion.

Key terms

  1. At-issue meaning—primary topic or literal event described by the utterance.
    1. ex. 'Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries.'—the literal event described.

Sense and Denotation

Dual interpretation of meaning: mental representation versus external world entity.

Key terms

  1. Sense—mental representation or lexical semantics held in the speaker's mind.
    1. ex. Thinking about botanical definitions or visual images of a strawberry.
  2. Denotation—actual object or entity in the world identified by a term.
    1. ex. 'Ode’imin' and 'strawberry' pointing to the same physical fruit.

Non-at-issue meaning

Content outside the surface-level literal claim, not targeted by sentential negation.

Key terms

  1. Non-at-issue meaning—content not constituting the main topic (e.g., implicatures and presuppositions).
    1. ex. Implicatures and presuppositions.

Truth-conditional meaning

Technical terminology for at-issue meaning used to verify factual truth or falsity.

Key terms

  1. Truth-conditional meaning—content used to verify claims; targets for "that is false" responses.
    1. ex. The part of a statement a supervisor rejects when saying "That is not the case."
  2. Non-truth-conditional meaning—misleading synonym for non-at-issue meaning (confusing as presuppositions require prior truth).

Presupposition

Information requiring acceptance as fact prior to utterance for semantic coherence.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition—background facts assumed true for a sentence to be meaningful.
    1. ex. The existence of a department head in 'The department head forgot to sign.'

Implicature

Suggested non-entailment derived from context rather than logical requirement.

Key terms

  1. Implicature—meaning suggested by an utterance that is not necessarily true in all contexts.
    1. ex. 'Mounissa bought 10lbs of strawberries' implying jam production.

Speaker commitment

Sincerity or obligation assumed by the speaker regarding discourse truth.

Key terms

  1. Speaker commitment—responsibility for the truth of an utterance during the discourse.
    1. ex. 'I didn't mean it' as a mechanism to retract a previous commitment.

Presupposition vs Implicature

Distinction based on semantic behavior under negation: survival versus cancellability.

Key terms

  1. Survival—presupposition property where meaning remains true despite main sentence negation.
  2. Cancellability—implicature property allowing retraction or change when the sentence is negated.

Diagnostics

Negation tool used to identify at-issue meaning and distinguish it from non-at-issue content.

Key terms

  1. Sentential negation—use of "it is not the case that..." to cancel at-issue meaning.
    1. ex. If meaning is canceled, it is at-issue; if it survives, it is non-at-issue (presupposition).

Cancellable Meanings

Retraction of suggested meanings without generating logical contradictions.

Key terms

  1. Cancellability—ability to take back suggestions by adding clarifying information.
    1. ex. 'She has three kids—in fact four' avoiding logical clash.

Contextual Influence

Situational knowledge dictating the specific interpretation of a linguistic utterance.

Key terms

  1. Context-dependence—interpretation variation based on shared knowledge between speaker and hearer.
    1. ex. 'This is a book about China' implies positive or negative outlooks depending on interest.

Conversational Implicature

Suggested meanings arising from underlying rules of human interaction and cooperation.

Key terms

  1. Conversational implicature—inferences derived from general principles of communicative cooperation.

Logical Entailment

Necessarily true meaning immune to contextual cancellation or modification.

Key terms

  1. Entailment—meaning that cannot be canceled without creating a logical contradiction.
    1. ex. 'This is a book about China' necessarily entails 'This is a book.'

Scalar Implicature

Implicatures involving scales of quantity where choosing a point implies excluding higher values.

Key terms

  1. Scalar implicature—suggested meaning where 'some' implies 'not all' or 'not more' on a scale.
    1. ex. Saying 'some' implies the higher value 'all' is false.

Implicature and context

Environmental factors causing non-entailed meaning to shift across different discourse settings.

Key terms

  1. Contextual shifting—process where natural understanding changes based on external facts or hearer preferences.
    1. ex. Book recommendation flipping based on whether a friend is a history enthusiast or not.

Maxim violations

Covertly breaking conversational rules, resulting in anomalous discourse and required repair.

Key terms

  1. Violation—quiet rule-breaking causing discourse breakdown without the hearer's immediate awareness.
    1. ex. Bo claiming to live in Ottawa then Toronto without explanation.
  2. Repair—process of straightening out facts after detecting a maxim violation.

Flouting maxims

Overt and deliberate rule-breaking intended to generate a specific, noticeable implicature.

Key terms

  1. Flouting—appearing to violate rules obviously to signal sarcasm or non-literal meaning.
    1. ex. Aya saying 'Toronto, Saskatchewan' to sarcastically imply the true location is obvious.

Communicative strategies

Methods ensuring intended meaning is conveyed and non-literal intent is recognized.

Key terms

  1. Directness—explicitly stating implied meaning to avoid literal misinterpretation in text.
    1. ex. Following a sarcastic email with a direct explanation.
  2. Non-verbal cues—intonation, winks, or facial expressions signaling that a speaker is flouting.

Quality Maxim

Gricean requirement that speakers provide truthful and consistent information during discourse.

Names and studies

  1. Bo and Aya—case study demonstrating that inconsistent facts trigger repair.
  2. Grice—theorist who established that implicatures arise from cooperative rules like truthfulness.
