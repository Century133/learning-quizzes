At-issue meaning

At-issue meaning constitutes the primary, surface-level proposition that represents the main claim of a sentence.

Key terms

  1. At-issue meaning — the literal topic of discussion that provides the core assertion of an utterance.
    • Example: "Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries" — the claim that the event of buying will occur. (Diagnostic: At-issue meaning is specifically targeted by sentential negation, such as "It is not the case that...").

Sense and Denotation

The lexical meaning of words can be analyzed either as an internal mental representation or as an external reference to a real-world entity.

Key terms

  1. Sense — the mental representation or lexical semantics of a term held within a speaker's mind.
    • Example: A coworker visualizing a specific image of a strawberry or considering its botanical definition.
  2. Denotation — the relationship where a word points to a specific physical entity or object in the actual world.
    • Example: The Ojibwe word "ode’imin" and the English word "strawberry" both pointing to the same physical fruit.

Non-at-issue meaning

Non-at-issue meaning refers to content that is conveyed by an utterance but exists outside the main, surface-level assertion.

Key terms

  1. Non-at-issue meaning — content that does not constitute the primary topic of discussion and is not the target of sentential negation.
    • Example: Implicatures and presuppositions.

Truth-conditional meaning

In technical literature outside this textbook, at-issue content is frequently referred to as truth-conditional meaning.

Key terms

  1. Truth-conditional meaning — the specific content used to verify the truth or falsity of a statement; the textbook avoids this label to prevent confusion with presuppositions, which also relate to truth.
    • Example: The specific step in a technical manual that a response of "that is false" would target.

Presupposition

A presupposition consists of information that must be accepted as true by the discourse participants for an utterance to make sense.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition — a type of non-at-issue meaning that is assumed to be true and "survives" sentential negation.
    • Example: "The department head forgot to sign" — necessarily assumes a specific department head exists, regardless of whether they forgot or not.

Speaker commitment

Speaker commitment involves the sincerity and discourse obligations a speaker assumes regarding the truth of their utterances.

Key terms

  1. Speaker commitment — the communicative obligation or literal intent a speaker expresses toward their statement during a conversation.
    • Example: Retracting a previous claim about coffee being as tasty as tea by stating, "I didn't mean it."

Implicature

Implicatures are suggested meanings that are not logically necessitated by the specific words used in an utterance.

Key terms

  1. Implicature — a non-entailment suggested by a sentence that is fundamentally dependent on the specific context of the conversation.
    • Example: "Mounissa bought 10lbs of strawberries" — suggesting she is making a large amount of jam.

Cancellable Meanings

Cancellability is a property of suggested meanings that allows them to be retracted without creating a logical contradiction.

Key terms

  1. Cancellability — the ability to retract an implicature or suggested meaning by providing further explicit clarification.
    • Example: "This is a book about the history of China, but it is not the case that this book is long."

Contextual Influence

The interpretation of an utterance's implicatures is heavily dictated by the environment and the shared knowledge between speakers.

Key terms

  1. Context-dependence — the way an implicature shifts based on the speaker's knowledge of the listener's preferences or the situation.
    • Example: Telling a history fan a book is about history implies they will like it, whereas telling a history hater the same thing implies they will not.

Conversational Implicature

Conversational implicatures are suggested meanings that arise specifically from the underlying rules and expectations of human cooperation.

Key terms

  1. Conversational implicature — a meaning suggested by a speaker that relies on the listener's assumption that the speaker is following rules of cooperation.
    • Example: Danny tells Josh, "This is a book about the history of China." Because Danny knows Josh is a history enthusiast, this implies Josh will enjoy the book.

Logical Entailment

Logical entailments are consequences that are necessarily true if the sentence is true and cannot be retracted under any circumstances.

Key terms

  1. Entailment — a necessary consequence of a sentence that is context-independent and cannot be cancelled.
    • Example: "This is a book about the history of China" necessarily entails "This is a book." (Attempting to cancel it results in a logical contradiction: ⊥ "...but it is not the case that this is a book.")

Flouting maxims

Flouting is the act of obviously and deliberately appearing to violate a conversational rule to prompt the listener to find a hidden meaning.

Key terms

  1. Flouting — the blatant violation of a maxim intended to be noticed by the listener to create an implicature.
    • Example: Using an exaggerated tone of voice or a wink to say something obviously false, signaling sarcasm.

Maxim violations

A maxim violation occurs when a speaker quietly fails to follow conversational rules in a way that is not intended to be noticed.

Key terms

  1. Violating — failing to follow a maxim covertly, which leads to uncooperative discourse and often requires a "Wait, what?" repair.
    • Example: Providing contradictory information about where one lives (e.g., Ottawa vs. Toronto) without signaling a joke, causing the conversation to break down.

Quality Maxim

The Maxim of Quality is the conversational requirement that participants be truthful and sincere in their contributions.

Key terms

  1. Maxim of Quality — the rule that speakers must tell the truth and only provide information for which they have adequate evidence.
    • Example: A speaker providing conflicting facts about their location, which violates this maxim and halts the progress of the discourse.

Communicative strategies

Communicative strategies describe the varying methods individuals use to navigate discourse and interpret implicature.

Key terms

  1. Discourse strategies — the methods used to convey meaning, which may differ among speakers regarding the use of sarcasm or indirectness.
    • Example: Clarifying a sarcastic remark out loud (e.g., "I was being sarcastic") for a listener who does not utilize implicature-based strategies.
