8.1 The Pectoral Girdle

Pectoral girdle mobility

The structural independence of the two pectoral girdles and their single, mobile attachment to the axial skeleton provide the upper limbs with an extensive and versatile range of motion.

Key terms

  1. Pectoral girdle — The two-bone structure, consisting of the scapula and clavicle, that attaches the upper limb to the axial skeleton.
  2. Mobility — The extensive range of motion enabled by the independent operation of each girdle and a single, mobile joint connection.

Clavicle function

The clavicle acts as a lateral strut supported by superior muscles to position the shoulder joint away from the body trunk, providing maximal freedom of motion for the limb while protecting underlying structures.

Key terms

  1. Strut — A lateral extension supported by muscles that holds the shoulder joint away from the body trunk.
    1. Maximal freedom of motion — The lateral positioning provided by the clavicle allows the upper limb a wide range of movement and manipulation.

Sternoclavicular joint

This joint serves as the primary anchor point between the appendicular and axial skeletons and is the sole bony articulation for the upper limb.

Key terms

  1. Sternal end of the clavicle — The triangular medial end of the bone that articulates with the manubrium of the sternum.
  2. Sternoclavicular joint — The only bony articulation between the pectoral girdle and the axial skeleton.
    1. Upward/downward and anterior/posterior — The specific directions of movement permitted by this joint during shoulder activity.

Scapular attachment

The scapula is suspended on the posterior thorax through complex muscular attachments rather than through direct articulation with the ribs of the thoracic cage.

Key terms

  1. Scapula — A flat, triangular bone located on the posterior aspect of the shoulder.
  2. Muscular attachment — The method of support where the bone is surrounded by muscle and does not join the ribs.

Force transmission

The clavicle serves as a vital conduit that conducts physical stress and impact from the upper limb directly to the sternum and the central axial skeleton.

Key terms

  1. Force transmission — The process of sending physical impact from the upper limb to the sternum.

Glenohumeral joint

The glenohumeral joint, commonly known as the shoulder joint, is the articulation formed where the scapula receives the head of the humerus.

Key terms

  1. Glenoid cavity — The shallow depression on the scapula that receives the humerus.
  2. Glenohumeral joint — The articulation between the scapula and the arm bone (humerus).

Clavicle fracture

Commonly caused by falls on outstretched arms or lateral shoulder impacts, a clavicular fracture causes the shoulder to drop and be pulled anteriorly and medially.

Key terms

  1. Fracture mechanism — A break resulting from force exerted when falling on outstretched arms or receiving a strong blow to the lateral shoulder.
  2. Complete fracture — A total break where the weight of the limb causes the shoulder to drop and muscles pull the shoulder and lateral fragment anteriorly and medially.

Inferior scapular angle

The inferior angle is the lowest point of the scapula and serves as a critical attachment site for powerful muscles that move the shoulder and upper limb.

Key terms

  1. Inferior angle of the scapula — The lowest corner of the bone, located where the medial and lateral borders meet.
    1. Muscle attachment point — A site for several powerful muscles involved in executing intensive shoulder and limb movements.

Vascular protection

The clavicle is positioned to safeguard the essential nerves and blood vessels as they pass from the body trunk into the upper limb.

Key terms

  1. Protective positioning — The location of the clavicle overlying the neurovascular structures that serve the upper limb.

Costoclavicular ligament

The costoclavicular ligament provides indirect support to the medial pectoral girdle by anchoring the clavicle to the first rib.

Key terms

  1. Costoclavicular ligament — The tissue spanning the sternal end of the clavicle and the underlying first rib.

Clavicle morphology

The clavicle is a horizontal, S-shaped bone that displays physical variations based on sex and the musculoskeletal demands of manual labor.

Key terms

  1. S-shape — The horizontal, curved long bone morphology of the clavicle.
  2. Sex differences — Morphological variations where male clavicles are typically heavier, longer, and more curved than female clavicles.
    1. Manual workers — Individuals who often exhibit more pronounced muscle attachment features and rougher bone surfaces where muscles attach.

Acromial end

The acromial end is the lateral articulation point of the collarbone that joins with the scapula to form the bony tip of the shoulder.

Key terms

  1. Acromial end of the clavicle — The lateral end of the bone that joins with the acromion of the scapula.
  2. Bony tip of the shoulder — The palpable landmark formed by the acromion where it joins the lateral end of the clavicle.

Scapular tubercles

These small bony projections are located immediately above and below the glenoid cavity to serve as anchors for the muscles of the arm.

Key terms

  1. Supraglenoid tubercle — The small bump located immediately above the glenoid cavity.
  2. Infraglenoid tubercle — The small bump located immediately below the glenoid cavity.

Scapular borders

The triangular shape of the scapula is defined by three margins that designate its spatial orientation on the posterior shoulder.

Key terms

  1. Superior border — The top margin of the scapula, which contains the suprascapular notch.
  2. Medial border — The inner margin of the scapula.
  3. Lateral border — The outer margin of the scapula.

Limb functional demands

Human evolutionary stance has dictated that the lower limbs specialize in weight-bearing stability while the upper limbs are adapted for high mobility and object manipulation.

Key terms

  1. Weight-bearing support — The primary function of the lower limb bones, which are adapted for stability and locomotion.
  2. High mobility — The primary demand for the upper limbs, allowing for a wide variety of activities and manipulation.
