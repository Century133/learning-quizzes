Ch6 Exam · 6.2–6.3

Actor-observer difference

This bias involves attributing others' behavior to internal traits while explaining one's own actions through situational factors.

Key terms

  1. Actor-observer difference: Bias favoring situational attributions for self and trait attributions for others.

Self: Situational focus driven by the high visual salience of external circumstances for the actor.

Others: Trait focus arising from the observed person being the primary center of visual attention.

Names and studies

  1. Nisbett, Caputo, Legant, & Marecek (1973): Participants used situational terms for self more than for fathers or Walter Cronkite.

Self-serving attributions

Motivational distortions protect self-esteem by leading individuals to internalize success and externalize failure.

Key terms

  1. Self-serving attributions: Bias favoring a positive self-image via internal credit and external blame.

Teacher example: Crediting teacher ability for high student scores but student lack of effort for failures.

Names and studies

  1. Mezulis, Abramson, Hyde, & Hankin (2004): Identified the motivational desire for self-enhancement as the driver for attributional distortion.

Fundamental attribution error

Perceivers systematically overestimate personality traits and underestimate situational impact when judging the behavior of others.

Key terms

  1. Fundamental attribution error (correspondence bias): Systematic overemphasis on internal traits over context when judging others.

Diner example: Judging a tipper as inherently generous rather than socially motivated to impress companions.

Names and studies

  1. Skitka, Mullen, Griffin, Hutchinson, & Chamberlin (2002): Observers rated quizmasters as smarter than contestants despite known random role assignment.

Weiner's model

Causal inferences regarding success and failure are categorized by the dimensions of locus and stability.

Key terms

  1. Locus: Dimension determining if causes are personal/internal or situational/external.

Personal causes: Internal factors including ability (Stable) and motivation (Unstable).

Situational causes: External factors including luck (Unstable) and task difficulty (Stable).

  1. Stability: Dimension determining if a cause is permanent or likely to fluctuate over time.

Stable: Fixed causes expected to remain constant in future outcomes.

Unstable: Fluctuating causes subject to change over time.

Names and studies

  1. Weiner (1985): Framework helping individuals identify specific targets for future performance improvement.

Covariation principle

Situational causality is inferred by observing if a behavior changes predictably across different social contexts and time.

Key terms

  1. Covariation principle: Determining causality by assessing the relationship between behavior and context.
  2. Consistency information: Whether a specific situation always produces the same behavior from an actor.
  3. Distinctiveness information: Whether behavior occurs uniquely in one situation and not in others.
  4. Consensus information: Whether most people react similarly to the same situational context.

Names and studies

  1. Kelley (1967) / Cheng & Novick (1990): Defined patterns of consistency, distinctiveness, and consensus in social attribution.

Attributional accuracy

Social navigation is generally effective but accuracy degrades under high cognitive demand or visual salience.

Key terms

  1. Salience: Degree to which a person stands out, attracting primary causal focus.

Salience effect: Visual orientation makes the observed person seem like the primary cause.

  1. Spontaneous processing: Rapid, effortless personal attribution making without conscious deliberation.
  2. Cognitive load: Tiredness or distraction preventing effortful adjustment for situational factors.

Cognitive load effect: Insufficient situational correction leading to increased correspondence bias.

Role expectations

Personality attributions increase when behavior violates social norms or specific social role requirements.

Key terms

  1. Unexpected behavior: Deviant actions that catalyze dispositional rather than situational inferences.

Names and studies

  1. Jones, Davis, & Gergen (1961): Found 91% trait belief for extraverted astronauts vs. 45% for submariners.

Choice and attribution

Perceived freedom of choice increases the strength of dispositional inferences relative to situational ones.

Key terms

  1. Situational attribution: Causal inference to external pressures, prominent when behavior is coerced.

Names and studies

  1. Jones & Harris (1967): Participants inferred true beliefs even when informed essay topics were assigned.

Attributional categories

Causal inferences are primarily categorized by whether the cause is located within the person or the environment.

Key terms

  1. Personal (internal or dispositional) attribution: Belief that behavior stems from an actor's internal traits.
  2. Situational (external) attribution: Belief that behavior stems from environmental pressures or social context.

Personal attribution dominance

Personal explanations occur more frequently than situational ones due to human salience and automatic processing.

Key terms

  1. Spontaneous trait inference: Automatic trait identification occurring before situational correction.
  2. Visual focus: Perceptual tendency to focus on the person, making them more salient than context.
  3. Identification: Initial step of labeling behavior (e.g., "generous") prior to adjusting for situation.

Attribution types

Psychologists utilize specific dimensions to categorize the nature of causal explanations for events.

Key terms

  1. Internal vs. External: Origin within the person vs. the environmental context.
  2. Stable vs. Unstable: Permanent factors vs. those expected to change over time.
  3. Global vs. Specific: Universal application across domains vs. uniqueness to single events.

Causal attribution

Causal attribution is the foundational social-cognitive process of determining the underlying reasons for behavior.

Key terms

  1. Causal attribution: Process of determining causes of behavior to understand and communicate with others.

Cultural attribution differences

Cultural background dictates whether person perception prioritizes individual traits or situational contexts.

Key terms

  1. Individualistic cultures: Societies (US, Canada) prioritizing personal independence and responsibility.
  2. Collectivistic cultures: Societies (Japan, India) prioritizing relationships and environmental influence.

Names and studies

  1. Miller (1984): Personal vs. situational attribution differences emerged and increased as children aged.
  2. Masuda & Nisbett (2001): Japanese students remembered background context; Americans focused on salient foreground.
  3. Hong, Morris, Chiu, & Benet-Martínez (2000): Cultural icons shifted bicultural students' focus between person and situation.

Person perception

Unique interpretations of identical behaviors arise from perceiver variables and prior experiences.

Key terms

  1. Person perception: Active interpretation of behavior using observation and internal schemas.

Interpretation process: Current perceptions colored by prior experiences, attitudes, and expectations.

Names and studies

  1. Dornbusch et al. (1965): Descriptions by one perceiver overlap more than descriptions of one target by different perceivers.

Implicit personality theories

Underlying beliefs about trait stability influence whether observers prioritize personality or situations.

Key terms

  1. Entity theorists: Individuals believing traits are fixed, focusing on personal trait attributions.

Trait focus: Primary emphasis on internal characteristics when observing behaviors.

  1. Incremental theorists: Individuals believing personality is malleable, focusing on dynamic processes.

Situation focus: Primary emphasis on environmental and psychological change.

Names and studies

  1. Molden, Plaks, & Dweck (2006): Entity theorists failed to encode situational info under time pressure.
  2. Blackwell, Trzesniewski, & Dweck (2007): Incremental theorists improved math grades significantly more than entity theorists.

Attributional mental health

Habitual causal explanation patterns correlate significantly with psychological resilience or vulnerability to depression.

Key terms

  1. Attributional style: Habitual explanation of negative events along internal, stable, and global dimensions.
  2. Negative attributional style: Internal, stable, and global explanations for negative events leading to hopelessness.
  3. Learned helplessness: State of passivity and lack of escape via external, stable, and global attributions.
  4. Positive attributional style: External, unstable, and specific explanations for negative events maintaining health.

Names and studies

  1. Alloy, Abramson, & Francis (1999): Linked negative attributional styles to subsequent episodes of clinical depression.
  2. Abramson, Seligman, & Teasdale (1978): Dogs became passive and stopped escaping shocks after exposure to inescapable stimuli.

Self-handicapping strategies

Proactive excuse creation shields self-esteem from potential failure by providing external attributions.

Key terms

  1. Self-handicapping: Statements or behaviors creating external attributions to protect against failure threats.

Behavioral handicapping: Physical obstacles to performance, such as performance-inhibiting drugs.

Claimed inability: Self-reports of stress or time constraints used as performance excuses.

Names and studies

  1. Berglas & Jones (1978): Men chose performance-inhibiting drugs to provide external excuses for failure.
  2. Zuckerman & Tsai (2005): Correlated self-handicapping with lower life satisfaction and substance abuse.

Need for cognition

Individual differences in processing motivation determine the depth and thoroughness of social information analysis.

Key terms

  1. Need for cognition: Motivation to think carefully and fully about social contexts.

Strong need: Thoughtful processing leading to more causal attributions overall.

Weak need: Impulsive processing resulting in quick, spontaneous, and punitive attributions.

Names and studies

  1. Sargent (2004): Low need for cognition predicts support for punitive crime responses.

Perceiver influence

The current accessibility of specific schemas determines which characteristics are prioritized during person perception.

Key terms

  1. Cognitive accessibility: Ease with which specific concepts or traits come to a perceiver's mind.

Accessible traits: High priority dimensions like appearance, race, and athletic skills.

Names and studies

  1. Park (1986): Observers use consistent, idiosyncratic dimensions to describe diverse social targets.
