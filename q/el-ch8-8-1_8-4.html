Ch8 Exam · 8.1, 8.4

At-issue meaning

At-issue meaning constitutes the literal, surface-level content that functions as the primary topic or "issue" under discussion in an utterance.

Key terms

  1. At-issue meaning — the literal, surface content that represents the main topic or "issue" being discussed.
    1. ex. 'Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries' — the primary event described by the sentence.

Sense and Denotation

Linguistic meaning can be systematically divided into the mental concepts held by a speaker and the specific real-world references those concepts point toward.

Key terms

  1. Sense — the lexical semantics or mental concept of a word held in a speaker's head.
    1. ex. the botanical definition or visual image of a "strawberry" in a person's mind.
  2. Denotation — the specific object or set of objects that a word points to in the actual world.
    1. ex. 'ode’imin' and 'strawberry' pointing to the same physical fruit.

Non-at-issue meaning

Non-at-issue meaning refers to any content that is suggested, implied, or assumed rather than being part of the literal surface claim.

Key terms

  1. Non-at-issue meaning — content that is suggested or assumed rather than being part of the literal, surface statement.
    1. ex. implicatures and presuppositions.

Truth-conditional meaning

Truth-conditional meaning is a technical term for at-issue content that determines the truth or falsity of a sentence and is the specific target of a "No, that is false" response.

Key terms

  1. Truth-conditional meaning — a term used outside the textbook to refer to at-issue meaning because it determines the truth or falsity of the sentence.
    1. ex. the content targeted by a supervisor saying "That is not the case" to reject a literal claim.

Presupposition

A presupposition is information that must be accepted as a background fact by discourse participants for an utterance to be logically coherent.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition — information that is assumed to be true already before a sentence is even uttered.
    1. ex. the use of 'the' in 'the department head' assuming a specific head exists.

Speaker commitment

Speaker commitment involves the discursive responsibility and sincerity a person adopts regarding the truth of their utterances.

Key terms

  1. Speaker commitment — the responsibility a person takes for the sincerity or truth of what they say during a discourse.
    1. ex. saying "coffee is just as tasty as tea" and meaning it sincerely.

Implicature

An implicature is a non-literal suggestion generated by an utterance based on the specific context of the conversation rather than logical entailment.

Key terms

  1. Implicature — a non-entailment suggested by a sentence based on the specific context of the conversation.
    1. ex. 'Mounissa bought 10lbs of strawberries' implying she is making jam.

Retraction

Retraction is the formal act of withdrawing a previously established speaker commitment or clarifying that a suggested non-at-issue meaning was unintended.

Key terms

  1. Retraction — the act of withdrawing a previous commitment or clarifying that a non-at-issue meaning was not intended.
    1. ex. saying "I didn't mean it that way" after suggesting a developer is careless.

Presupposition survival

Presupposition survival is the linguistic phenomenon where background assumptions remain true even if the main at-issue claim is denied through negation.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition survival — the phenomenon where a presupposition remains true even when the sentence is negated.
    1. ex. "It is not the case that Mounissa will buy the discounted strawberries" still requiring that discounted strawberries exist.

At-issue diagnostics

Linguists employ diagnostic tools like sentential negation to isolate the literal claim of a sentence from its non-at-issue components.

Key terms

  1. Sentential negation — the use of "it is not the case that..." to identify at-issue meaning.
    1. ex. identifying that the literal claim 'Mounissa will buy strawberries' is what gets canceled by negation.

Sense vs Denotation

The distinction between sense and denotation highlights how different linguistic labels can share the same real-world referent while maintaining unique internal definitions.

Key terms

  1. Sense vs Denotation — the contrast between the internal definition of a word and the external object it describes.
    1. ex. 'Ode’imin' and 'strawberry' having different linguistic senses but the same denotation.

Sense

Sense refers specifically to the cognitive aspect of meaning, encompassing the internal mental images or botanical definitions a speaker associates with a word.

Key terms

  1. Sense — the mental lexical semantics or "image" associated with a word in a speaker's mind.
    1. ex. thinking about a botanical definition while discussing a fruit.

Retraction and Implicature

Speakers utilize clarification as a preventative measure to ensure that a listener does not interpret a suggested implicature as a settled speaker commitment.

Key terms

  1. Clarification — the use of additional phrases to ensure a listener does not accept a suggested implicature as fact.
    1. ex. adding "but I still need to write the conclusion" to ensure the listener does not assume the report is finished.

Cancellable Meanings

Cancellable meanings are non-entailed suggestions that can be explicitly denied by the speaker without creating a logical contradiction.

Key terms

  1. Cancellability — the property of a meaning that allows it to be taken back or denied.
    1. ex. "This book is about China, but it is not long."

Contextual Influence

Implicatures are heavily influenced by background knowledge, such as a speaker’s awareness of a listener’s specific hobbies or preferences.

Key terms

  1. Context — the specific circumstances or background knowledge (like a friend's hobbies) that determine which implicature is generated.
    1. ex. a sentence about a history book implying "you will like this" because Danny knows Josh is a history enthusiast.

Conversational Implicature

Conversational implicatures are meanings suggested by an utterance that arise from the underlying cooperative rules humans follow during interaction.

Key terms

  1. Conversational implicature — a meaning suggested by an utterance that arises specifically from conversational rules.
    1. ex. concluding a friend will like a book based on their known interests and the cooperative nature of the reply.

Logical Entailment

An entailment is a component of meaning that must necessarily be true if the sentence is true and cannot be cancelled without contradiction.

Key terms

  1. Entailment — a meaning that is necessarily true and cannot be canceled without creating a logical contradiction.
    1. ex. 'This is a book about China' necessarily entailing 'This is a book.'

Scalar Implicature

Scalar implicature occurs when a speaker's choice of a weaker term on a scale implies that the stronger, more informative term is false.

Key terms

  1. Scalar implicature — a type of suggestion where using a weaker term implies the denial of a stronger term.
    1. ex. using "some" to imply "not all."

Cancellability

The cancellability test is the primary diagnostic for distinguishing implicatures from entailments by checking if a meaning can be denied without contradiction.

Key terms

  1. Cancellability test — the ability to add "but it is not the case that..." to a sentence without it being a contradiction.
    1. ex. "She has three kids — in fact four" showing that "exactly three" was a cancellable implicature.

Context dependence

Context dependence describes the instability of implicatures, which may shift or disappear entirely depending on the conversational environment or audience.

Key terms

  1. Context dependence — the characteristic of implicatures where they change or disappear depending on the environment.
    1. ex. an entailment about a book remains constant, while the implicature of "liking" it changes based on the audience's interests.

Conversational Rules

Conversational rules provide the logical and cooperative framework that allows discourse participants to derive non-literal meanings from factual statements.

Key terms

  1. Conversational rules — the underlying principles used by speakers and listeners to interact and derive non-literal meaning.
    1. ex. the framework that allows a listener to conclude a recommendation from a description.

Entailment vs Implicature

The difference between entailment and implicature is defined by whether a meaning is logically necessary or merely suggested and subject to retraction.

Key terms

  1. Entailment vs Implicature — the distinction between meanings that are logically required and those that are merely suggested and cancellable.
    1. ex. a history book is always a book (entailment), but it is not always long (implicature).

Negation and Implicature

Sentential negation targets at-issue meaning because non-at-issue content, such as presupposition, must be true before the sentence is uttered and is thus not subject to denial.

Key terms

  1. Negation impact — the fact that sentential negation targets at-issue meaning but might not cancel the implicature.
    1. ex. Mounissa not buying strawberries (at-issue canceled) while still potentially liking them (implicature survives).

Context

Context encompasses the surrounding discourse environment and shared situational knowledge required to interpret non-entailed suggestions.

Key terms

  1. Context — the environment and shared knowledge that allows a non-entailment to be suggested.
    1. ex. the situational environment where Danny and Josh discuss a book to determine if it is a recommendation.

Presupposition vs Implicature

Presuppositions are background requirements that survive negation, whereas implicatures are context-driven suggestions that can be cancelled without logical conflict.

Key terms

  1. Presupposition vs Implicature — the difference between what is assumed true beforehand and what is suggested by context.
    1. ex. the existence of a department head (presupposition) vs the idea that a book is long (implicature).

Names and studies

  1. Grice (1975) — developed the theory of logic and conversation, proposing that interaction is a cooperative endeavor governed by rules where implicatures are cancellable without contradiction.
